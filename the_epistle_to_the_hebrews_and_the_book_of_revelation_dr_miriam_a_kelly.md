# The Epistle to the Hebrews and The Book of Revelation
**Author:** [[Dr. Miriam A Kelly]]

## 1. Verbatim Source Text
### The Nature of Hebrews

It can be suggested that the Epistle to the Hebrews stands alone in the writings of the NT because of the particular way in which it presents Christ and his work as the fulfilment of all that was prefigured in the OT. Since there is no indication at the beginning of Hebrews that it was addressed to any particular audience, there are scholars who suggest that this work ought not to be properly regarded as a letter. In fact, it is not until ch.13 that the author indicates that he is writing to a specific group and the epistolary style becomes apparent. It may well have been a series of homilies adapted for the present need. (F.F.Bruce, The Epistle to the Hebrews, NICNT, Grand Rapids, Berdmans, rev. edn., 1990), 389.

### Authorship

The author of the Epistle to the Hebrews is unknown to us. The original recipients would have known him but from a very early date his identity has been hotly debated. For this reason, Hebrews was placed among the antilegomena (contested writings), which meant that it was not accepted into the Canon of Scripture for a considerable time. The main reason for this was the question of apostolic authorship. Several names have been associated with this letter including, Paul, Apollos and Barnabas. Whichever view of authorship is held, the matter of inspiration is not under question, and on this ground alone the place of Hebrews is secured in Scripture. (The main debate as to the Pauline authorship centres around the quality of the Greek found in this epistle. Some have suggested that Paul wrote the letter in Hebrew and that Luke translated it into Greek. The title in the Authorised Version 'The Epistle of Paul to the Hebrews' is not supported by any of the earliest manuscripts). "Who wrote the epistle God only knows certainly" (Origen, Eusebius VI.25).

### Date of writing

M.C.Tenney shares some observations: "The epistle was written during the lifetime of the second generation of Christians (2:1-4) and at a considerable interval after the conversion of the recipients (5:12). They had forgotten the 'former days' (10:32) but their leaders had died (13:7). Timothy had been imprisoned (13:23), but was still living and had been liberated. The allusions to the priesthood imply that the temple was still standing, but the removal of Jewish institutions was not far too far distant (12:27). Persecution was imminent (10:32-36; 12:4). The epistle seems to fit best the situation of the late sixties, when the church at Rome feared persecution and when the fall of the Jewish commonwealth was imminent." (M.C.Tenney), 359. Hebrews must be dated before the end of the first century as Clement of Rome quotes from it in his epistle to the Corinthians (c. A.D. 95). Reference is made to the temple as if it was still standing (8:4,13; 10:11; 13:10,11).

### Occasion and Purpose

Since the authorship is in question and there is no actual mention in the text itself that the epistle is written to 'the Hebrews', the reason for the letter has to be drawn from the contents, or internal evidence. (The title 'to Hebrews' dates from the earliest manuscripts, but this name appears nowhere in the body of the text. The content is clearly addressed to those who were enamoured with the Levitical code and the Mosaic Law. For this reason the title 'To Hebrews' has been maintained). It is most likely that 'Hebrews' is addressed to Christians who may have ethnic Jews, although some dispute this suggestion. Most of what is said about relapsing into Judaism and returning to the law only makes sense if the readers had at one time been converted out of Judaism. Whoever they were they had been acquainted with the gospel and had heard it preached by eyewitnesses of Jesus' life (2:3-4). There is every indication that these 'Hebrews' were having second thoughts about their Christian faith. They could not reconcile their 'Jewishness' with what was happening to them. They were suffering considerable persecution (10:32-34) and appear confused about their Christian beliefs. Some of their leaders had died (13:7), and they appear to think that if they returned to Mosaic Law they would be spared the difficulties of the present. They may have believed that their suffering was totally due to their identification with Christ and Christians. The book of Hebrews presents to the reader the transition that takes place from the old covenant Levitical system to that which now reigns under the New Covenant; Christ himself has ushered the latter in. It is perhaps a goal of the author to show how all that is now established under Christ was prefigured under the Old Covenant in images and types. Since the reality foretold under the Old is now realised, it makes no sense to return to the 'shadows' (Heb 10:1). This book shows on numerous occasions that Christ is far superior to anything the Jews knew in the past and that there was only one way to move forward and that was by faith in Christ. Any attempt to reinstate the Levitical/legal system would be a step away from God, and away from the faith of the very Old Testament saints they admired.

### Contents

Hebrews compares the Old and new covenants demonstrating the superiority of the new. The examples taken throughout the book are those with which Hebrew Christians would have been familiar. The Old Testament in the Greek translation known as the Septuagint (LXX) is the text upon which the exposition is based. The central observation of Hebrews is that the entire system of the New Covenant installed through the sacrifice of Christ is Superior to that of the Old Covenant. He is Superior to the Angels (1:1-2:18); his priesthood is superior to that of Moses (3:1-4:13) and the Aaronic priesthood, which was only a figure of the heavenly as, was the earthly temple a shadow of the heavenly (4:14-8:5). Also the Covenant which is now in order is superior to that of the Old Covenant, which was based upon the law of take away sin (8:6-10:18). The way is now open for all to approach God through Sinai and its system; and Christ's sacrifice was the perfect, once for all, sacrifice to Christ while living in hope and spurring 'others on to love and good deeds' (10:19-25).

### Christology

This book is rich in Christology. Christ is presented in such a manner that he would be completely understood by those who were well versed in the Old Testament. The person and work of Christ is central. Christ is the Son who is the exact representation of the Father's being (1:3). He is the one of whom the Father says: 'Your throne, O God will last for ever and ever' (1:8). This is why he is superior to all created beings. In spite of his divinity he is fully identified with mankind in the equivalent of a Johannine incarnation and death (2:7,9,14). Christ is the great High Priest in the likeness of the evasive Melchizedek (4:14; 5:6,10; 7:1-3,11,17). (The Hebrew meaning of this name is 'King of Righteousness' or 'King of Justice'). Not only is he the perfect High Priest, but also he offers the perfect and only sufficient sacrifice for the sin of all mankind (4:15b; 5:9) because he does not need to offer sacrifices for his own sin since he is sinless and perfect (7:26,27). He lives eternally to intercede for all who come to God through him (7:25). The eternal Sonship of Christ is seen in the words, "Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and for ever" (13:8).

### Atonement

Christ is both priest and sacrifice. His work is finished (1:3). He offered one sacrifice, for all sins, for all time. It was 'once for all' (7:27). The New Covenant is explained and seen as a fulfilment of Jer 31:31-34. The nature of the New Covenant is vital. The author makes it very clear that the Levitical priesthood could not provide perfection for those who attempted to submit to it (7:11). Christ had become the guarantor of a better covenant (7:22) as he has an eternal priesthood (7:24). The New Covenant is a covenant of the Spirit who now dwells within each believer (10:15-17).

### The warnings regarding apostasy

These warnings are considerable. This apostasy may take several forms. There is a hint that some apostasy may merely be seen in terms of 'drifting away' (2:1) where carelessness prevails. A lack of spiritual growth and the failure to grasp the central truths of Christianity are at the centre of this (5:12-6:2). But also, believers who hear God's voice and fail to respond accordingly may well become hardhearted and disobedient (3:7-19).

### The examples of faith

In order to persevere in the face of suffering it is necessary to live a life of faith (10:35-39). Chapter 11 illustrates the importance of faith and gives a list of past men and women of faith. The example of these 'witnesses' is used, together with the section is understood in its entirety in the context of suffering and perseverance from 10:35 to 11:40. This mat underline the fact that even those who by faith accepted suffering and death were commended for their faith (11:39) as they awaited the promise of God. Suffering and discipline ought not to be shunned, as God allows this for the benefit of his sons (12:5-8).

### The nature of the church

The old priesthood is outmoded. All believers are viewed as priests able to enter the heavenly temple with spiritual sacrifices (13:15-16).

### Outline of Hebrews

The better messenger: the Son (1:1-2:18)
The better apostle (3:1-4:13)
The better priest (4:14-7:28)
The better covenant (8:1-9:28)
The better sacrifice (10:1-31)
The better way: faith (10:32-12:29)
Conclusion: the practice of faith (13:1-25)

Hebrews seems to fall into two main parts: The main argument (1:1-10:18) and the closing exhortation (10:19-13:25). Note 'exhortation' (13:22).

Exhortations (to urge, encourage or insist)
Hebrews contains several examples of exhortations:
Let us hold fast to our confession; Let us draw near to the throne; Let us press on to perfection; Let us draw near to God; Let us hold fast to the confession of our faith; Let us consider one another; Let us lay aside every weight; Let us run the race, looking to Jesus; Let us have grace; Let us go forth; and Let us offer up a sacrifice of praise (13:15).

### The Book of Revelation

"The book of Revelation closes the canon and the history of the NT. Irrespective of whether or not it was the last book to be written, it is final in its thoughts, for it embodies the expectation of a church that had been launched in the world as an institution, and that was eagerly awaiting the consummation of its mission." (M.C.Tenney), 371.

### Background to the Apocalypse

The experience of the apostle John
It is commonly held that the apostle John was exiled on the Island of Patmos (1:4,9) during the reign of emperor Domitian (A.D.81-96). Persecution was not widespread at the time, but the emperor's claim to worship anticipated it. There is lots of debate regarding the authorship of Hebrews. Revelation

The units of the Apocalypse
"The book consists of a series of visions, each of which is a unit in itself. While there are many small units introduced by the phrase 'and I saw' (5:1,11; 6:1,9, etc), the four main divisions are introduced by the phrase 'in the Spirit' (1:10; 4:2; 17:1-3; 21:9-10). These divisions are of varied content and unequal length, but they give a unitary organisation to the book." 390.

Apocalyptic literature
We need to understand what is meant by apocalyptic literature. (Tenney), 381-383, explains the genre this way:
"Revelation belongs to the category of apocalyptic literature. Apocalyptic literature was usually produced in times of persecution and oppression as a means of encouraging those who were suffering for their faith. It was characterised by (1) an intense despair of present circumstances and an equally intense hope of divine intervention in the future; (2) by the use of symbolic language, dreams, and visions; (3) by the introduction of celestial and demonic powers as messengers and agents in the progress of God's purpose; (4) by the prediction of a catastrophic judgment of the wicked and supernatural deliverance for the righteous; and (5) frequently by the pseudonymous ascription of the writing to a prominent character of Biblical history, such as Ezra or Enoch (Book of Enoch). The book of Revelation possesses most of these characteristics except that the author declares his name, and assumes that he is known, not as a past celebrity, but as a present participant in the affairs of those whom he addressed."

The apocalyptic presents ideas in bold strokes using images, types and symbols. It is necessary to understand that many visions, creatures, beasts, scrolls, etc as part of this descriptive language. Many of the images ought to be viewed in the same way, as one would interpret the significance of a caricature or cartoon. Not every detail is of significance but it is the message of the entire picture that is important. In the same way, the apocalyptic in the Revelation is like a word picture, which is pointing out the overall importance of what God or Satan is doing. A lot is descriptive of what may be taking place behind the scenes, sometimes in the heavenlies and sometimes on the earth. Difficulties over interpretation arise when the significance of each detail is extended further than was intended by the author. The reader may need to stand back and see the panoramic picture, not from the limited perspective of earth and time, but from God's position.

### The Interpretation of Revelation

When it comes to interpreting the Revelation the possibilities appear to be without bounds. It is necessary therefore to outline the four major schools of interpretation represented by modern scholars:

The Preterist school (prophecy for the time) holds that the symbolism of the book relates only to the events of the day in which it was written. Many liberal scholars rule out predictive prophecy (e.g., the Antichrist is not taken to be a future dictator, but a Roman Caesar of the time). Thus Revelation is a symbolic account of the events, which transpired between the Church and the Roman State at the end of the first century.

The Idealist or Spiritualists school (prophecy being fulfilled all the time) sees no need to look for historical links between the events described in Revelation and actual history. Chronology does not play a role, and everything that is recorded is to be understood in the light of the cosmic battles, which transpire between the forces of God and the forces of Satan. For this school, most of the book needs to be interpreted in a doctrinal and ethical manner so that everything, which is symbolic, needs to be spiritualised.

The Historicist school (prophecy through time) holds that the events described in Revelation are a survey of historical events, which transpired between the end of the apostolic age and the Parousia. They culminate with the return of Christ and the end of the age. Although there are no clear indications as to how the events are to be linked neither to chronology nor to actual historical equivalents. The old evangelicals A.J.Gordon and A.B.Simpson held this view.

The Futurist school (prophecy before time) holds that the first three chapters of Revelation apply either to the day in which the book was written, or else that the seven churches of Asia represent seven periods of church history (as do the historicists), but contend that from chapter four the remainder of the book deals with the period of the 'Great Tribulation' which takes place before Christ's return. Thus everything after chapter three should be understood as a prophecy, which describes future events. Bible expositors such as C.I. Scofield and H.A. Ironside are futurists.

Millennialist views based on Rev. 20
See Rev 20:1-7, which speaks of Christ reigning on the earth for a thousand years. A number of views are held with this passage in mind:

The amillennial view holds that the millennium does not (or will not) exist as a literal period. Christ may appear at any time, but his coming will mark the end of the age, with the final judgement, and the making of everything new.

### Summary

The premillennial view holds that Christ will return personally to rule over the kingdoms of the earth; that the dead will be raised and will reign with him for 1000 years; that at the end of this time there will be rebellion; then will come the final judgment and the eternal state.

The postmillennial view takes the 1000 years to be figurative of a long interval that precedes the coming of Christ. In this period the gospel triumphs over the nations, introducing the reign of peace that will endure until Christ returns in final judgment.

A careful reading of the book makes it evident that there are strands of truth, which relate to all the above views. The difficulty arises when the entire work is forced to fit one and only one of these options. For example, the first three chapters ought to be viewed in as some sense historical. It is addressed to actual churches, which had parallels between the state of various churches, and churches throughout history but one could argue that is not the initial meaning here. One ought not spiritualise the first three chapters without doing damage to basic hermeneutical principles. At the same time it is possible to suggest that there are indications within the various scenes depicted in the rest of Revelation, that there is cosmic conflict taking place. Some of these episodes may even fit into the views of the preterists and idealists but certainly not all of them.

Although the underlying literal method of interpretation is sufficient to understand much of the book, the nature of apocalyptic literature demands that room be made for the interpretation of the figurative and symbolic sections within the framework of the futuristic prophecy.

Finally, although the overall view of Revelation is futuristic, some of the events described in the body of the work may have since been fulfilled. Nevertheless, the final accomplishment of the overall plan depicted, awaits Christ's return and is therefore in the future, not only to John but also to ourselves.

### Methodology

Scholars vary greatly in the methodology of interpretation of this book. It has already been stated that the apocalyptic ingredient is what tends to throw the modern reader. The methodology adapted in the interpretation ought to incorporate any understanding of the structure of the work. If Revelation is understood to be a chronological record of events from beginning to end, the interpreter will most likely be confused by what they read. If however, it is understood that there is a progression toward the Parousia but that there is also a great deal of recapitulation from various perspectives so that there is considerable overlap and expansion of different themes, then the whole picture may take on more meaning.

### The Purpose of Revelation

The book commences with the words, 'The revelation of Jesus Christ, which God gave him to show his servants what must soon take place. He made it known by sending his angel to his servant John, who testifies to everything he saw - that is, the word of God and the testimony of Jesus Christ' (1:1-2). This underlines the fact that Christ is the central character of the book. All that is reported between the introduction and conclusion is part of the testimony concerning Jesus. This book is perhaps saying that no matter what happens, there is coming a day when all evil, all destruction. All hate and even all death will be done away with. The Christ, who is presently invisible, will be at the centre of everything when he comes. This is the theme of the book: the coming of Christ and the events surrounding this coming. His justice and reign will prevail. In effect, he is saying: Do not give up! 'Yes, I am coming soon' (22:20).

The book gives a revelation of Christ. From chapter one his holiness, authority and power can be felt. He is the Lion-Lamb in the midst of the throne, able to open the seals of the scroll (5:1-14). He is a powerful saviour, who redeems his people on earth, and keeps them safe to his bridal feast (19:7; 21:9). He is the King of kings and Lord of lords (19:9), and the book states clearly that time and history are in Christ's hands.

This vision would give to John, and the Christians suffering under the rule of the emperor, tremendous support and strength. The appreciation of the Lord's omnipotence, omniscience and omnipresence has given the church endurance in persecution down through the years. Perhaps the book will give fortitude to the church at the end of the age.

Note the last promise, prayer and benediction of the book, which perhaps provides a fitting conclusion to the NT and the Bible as a whole.

He who testifies to these things says,
"Yes, I am coming soon."
Amen. Come, Lord Jesus.
The grace of the Lord Jesus be with God's people.
Amen" (Rev.22: 20-21).

## 3. Thematic Analysis Matrices
### Theme: Superiority of Christ in Hebrews
The document argues that Christ is superior to the angels, Moses, and the Aaronic priesthood. His sacrifice is presented as the perfect, once-for-all fulfillment of the Old Covenant's shadows, rendering the Levitical system outmoded.

### Theme: Apocalyptic Genre
The author defines apocalyptic literature as a genre produced during persecution to provide hope through symbolic language, visions, and the promise of divine intervention and judgment, noting that Revelation should be interpreted as a panoramic 'word picture' rather than a strictly chronological record.

### Theme: Interpretive Schools of Revelation
The text outlines four major schools of interpretation: Preterist (historical events of the first century), Idealist (symbolic cosmic battles), Historicist (survey of church history), and Futurist (prophecy of future events like the Great Tribulation).
