### Publishers

We are told from a young age that you are not supposed to judge a book by its cover, but knowing who has published a particular source may indeed tell you if it is worth your time. Here is a list of publishers you will encounter, running roughly from more scholarly to less academic: University Press... (Cambridge, Oxford, Sheffield, Harvard, Yale, Chicago, etc.) Doubleday Abingdon Paulist Press Augsburg T&T Clark/Continuum Scholars Press Fortress Press Eerdmans Westminster/John Knox SPCK Baker Academic Paternoster InterVarsity Press (IVP) SCM Hendrickson Zondervan Baker Broadman & Holman Word Moody. This list is not exhaustive: it does not cover every publisher. Thus, you will need to do a little research and background reading to find out if a particular publisher is worth reading. This list is not etched in stone: there are a few newer Zondervan sources that are of very high quality and worth reading, and some older IVP sources that are frankly not academic at all. Moreover, this list is one man's interpretation: other teachers may disagree and you may come to find you do also. Read widely and use your own judgment. Your research should reflect the use of good, scholarly, critical sources (and critical is not a dirty word!). Therefore if at all possible your bibliography should contain more from the upper two-thirds of the list than from the bottom. Newer is not always better, but having more recent sources shows that you are incorporating recent scholarship and ideas in your work. Make sure a good percentage of your sources are relatively recent.

### Commentaries

It is important when doing academic research to distinguish between scholarly commentaries and devotional commentaries. Words in the title such as 'exposition', 'devotional', 'preacher' and 'sermon' will indicate that a commentary is not as suitable for academic purposes. This does not mean that these are worthless books! It means they cannot be the basis for your research. Consult D.A. Carson, New Testament Commentary Survey, and Tremper Longman III, Old Testament Commentary Survey, for more detailed interaction on these issues. Calvin's Commentaries (*****) Keil-Delitzsch Old Testament Commentary (***) The Biblical Illustrator (*) The Expositor's Bible Commentary (*) Young's Commentary (*) Matthew Henry (*) Critical and Experimental Commentary (*) The People's Bible (*) The Pulpit Commentary (*) The Wesleyan Bible Commentary (*) Expositions of Holy Scripture (*) Clarke's Commentary (*) Individual commentaries: Some commentaries are published outside of a series, on their own. You need to take extra caution to make sure such commentaries are useable for your research. Some of these individual commentaries (such as those by James Montgomery Boice, John Macarthur, or older sources such as those by Charles Spurgeon) are intended to be homiletical or expositional commentaries; this means they are meant for pastors writing sermons, not students writing essays. ### Types of Sources: Primary, Secondary, and Reference

There are several different types of resources available to you when doing research. Though these are not rigid distinctions, here is a broad overview of sources: Primary: Sources that contain original, non-interpreted or non-evaluated information. This can be almost anything, depending on your field of research. Secondary: Sources that analyse, evaluate and interpret the information contained within primary sources. These tend to be argumentative. Usually books, articles, and other writings by scholars and researchers. Reference (Tertiary): Sources that compile, analyse, and digest secondary sources. These tend to be factual, and include encyclopaedias, indexes, textbooks, dictionaries, introductions, etc. Work cycle When researching, your approach will appear backwards: 1. Start with reference material to find out background information, summaries, and bibliographies of primary and secondary sources. 2. Find books, articles and other secondary sources providing more thorough analyses of your topic. Check to see what other scholars have been saying. 3. Having gained a solid background on your topic, you are better able to understand, interpret, and analyse the primary source information. Your research should show the use of all three types of sources in your bibliographies and footnotes.

## 3. Thematic Analysis Matrices
### Theme: Source Hierarchy
The document emphasizes a hierarchy of sources, categorizing publishers and reference works by their academic rigor. It advises students to prioritize 'more scholarly' sources over 'less critical' ones, while noting that this is not an absolute rule but a guide for research quality.

### Theme: Academic vs. Devotional Commentary
A clear distinction is made between scholarly commentaries (intended for academic research) and devotional/homiletical commentaries (intended for sermon preparation). The text warns that titles containing 'exposition', 'devotional', 'preacher', or 'sermon' are generally unsuitable for academic essays.

### Theme: Research Methodology
The document outlines a 'work cycle' for research that moves from reference materials (tertiary) to secondary sources (analysis/interpretation) and finally to primary sources (original data). It stresses that students must engage directly with primary sources rather than relying solely on secondary interpretations.

### Theme: Critical Evaluation of Sources
The text encourages students to exercise discernment, noting that 'critical' is not a negative term in academia. It advises against relying on older, outdated sources unless they are foundational (like Calvin) and emphasizes the importance of incorporating recent scholarship.
