# Truth, Method, and Church Growth: A Response to David E. Wells
**Author:** [[Robert Weathers]]

## 1. Verbatim Source Text
### Introduction

In a recent spate of books on church growth, writers are advising a change in the perspective from which most local church methodologies derive. Reflecting on church growth trends that began in the 1970s, these writers argue that many churches in the West are still using evangelistic methods that were built on what are now an outdated set of assumptions. In the era which lasted, roughly, until the end of the 1950s, churches assumed that America was largely a nation of believers, populating a culture in which the Christian church was only moderately threatened in doctrine and practice. Methods and assumptions that may have been effective then are still being used by churches today. Now, though, those methods are hampering the growth of otherwise healthy churches. These writers recognize that a radical intellectual and cultural shift has affected Western culture since the 1950s, and with it the cultural mind has shifted as well. As a result of the shifting worldview of the West, people think and live by a set of assumptions that is culturally foreign to many believers in traditional evangelical churches. Now a paradox exists. Although "the United States has become the largest mission field in the Western hemisphere, and most of the countries of Europe are almost secular wastelands," many evangelical congregations are finding it increasingly difficult to grasp this mindset that drives the assumptions and attitudes of their unsaved neighbors. Consequently, believers either defend the status quo or relinquish much hope in making a decisive impact for Christ in their communities. To answer this problem, church growth methodologists are counseling American evangelicals to return to the indigenous principle of missions. At the heart of this principle is the integration of form and content. That is, if evangelism is to be successful in a given culture, the church must first see itself from the unchurched person's point of view. Then the local church attempts to present the gospel and its life-changing content in forms that the unchurched will understand. For the evangelical church to be successful in the next century, the church will need to return to this principle, first articulated by the Apostle Paul with his directive to be "all things to all people" (1 Cor. 9:22). However, two inseparable factors inherent in the presentation of the gospel in culture-relevant forms complicate the implementation of this principle: (1) the nature of the cultural and intellectual shift that has reshaped the ideological landscape of our nation, and (2) how much, in fact, the church must accommodate the culture in order to reach it. A consideration of these factors within the context of current church growth trends shows that these writers are correct in their admonitions toward change in the local church.

### The Shift to the Postmodern West

Most of the voices in the current debate stress the practical aspects of church growth and the changes that the church must make to reach people in the next century. Few, however, grapple with the philosophical and theological underpinnings of our culture that make these changes necessary. The shift in the cultural and intellectual milieu that church growth methodologists are observing is the shift from modernity to postmodernity. Although scholars disagree on when this shift began, they generally regard the most significant changes to have begun in America after World War II. The Enlightenment paradigm, with its modernist optimism, was fading into obscurity, having been discredited by the atrocities of war and the failures of modern science. Humanity emerged from the war seeking new ideologies to anchor a floundering worldview. The Baby Boom hit, and its children came of age in the counter-cultural revolution of the 1960s. When America emerged from this era, its cultural worldview was being defined by relativism and a new spirituality, while simultaneously suffering from the modernist residue of an anti-supernaturalism. Furthermore, the extensive pluralization of the West rapidly reshaped the ideological landscape of the nation. In light of this cultural condition, church growth methodologists counsel that the local church rethink its methods if it is to reach late- and post-modernists. Needed, as well, they say is a careful consideration of the integration of truth content into postmodern forms. Following the advice of these church growth methodologists, some churches have adopted an approach that is showing astounding success. Proponents call this a "seeker-targeted" approach, a methodology which, in my view, is simply an application of the missionary paradigm to a culture awash in late-modern and postmodern mores.

### The Seeker-Targeted Church

Of the churches that have adopted a seeker-targeted paradigm, Willow Creek Community Church in South Barrington, Illinois, stands out as the pioneer. Planted in suburban Chicago by pastor Bill Hybels in 1975, Willow Creek was the product of his conviction that the people of contemporary Chicago would respond to the gospel if it was presented in forms with which they could readily identify. He deliberately sought to target suburban people who were unreachable by the methods of the traditional church. The astounding achievement of the Willow Creek paradigm has spawned duplicate efforts throughout the nation, with varying degrees of success. I believe that the seeker-oriented church will define church growth for the next generation because the seeker model takes seriously the changes that have occurred in American culture and uses varying forms accordingly to communicate biblical truth. Nevertheless, the key to the theological and practical success of the paradigm remains the degree to which the essence of the gospel message is held intact while presented in forms understandable to postmodern people. In fact, some critics express caution, or even outrage, at how closely they believe the model brings a pastor to cultural accommodation. They charge that the very success of the seeker-targeted movement is proof that its proponents have already acquiesced to culture and betrayed the essence of the gospel.

### David Wells' Lamentation

One such critic, David Wells, has raised challenges that cannot be ignored by any church growth advocate teaching methods for reaching a postmodern culture. In two profound works, he contests the assumptions and practices of evangelical churches trying to reach the contemporary late-modern or postmodern world. His critiques have defined the climate for the discussion of church growth in the postmodern context. In No Place for Truth, Wells laments the loss of the "theological center" of the evangelical church. He describes theology as "a knowledge that belongs first and foremost to the people of God" so that "the proper and primary audience is therefore the Church, not the learned guild." Therefore, theological unity should exist among evangelical churches. Yet this unity, and the theological acuity of the pastor, is what Wells believes has been lost with the onslaught of modernity and secularism in the West. Wells argues that the healthy theology that once guided the work of the local church has been pushed to the periphery as evangelicals have adopted the assumptions and values of modernity. He blames the Church Growth Movement for causing the local church pastor to displace theology in favor of methods that result in visible success. As a result, the christian ministry has become a profession in competition with secular professions for worldly status. Without reservation, Wells charges "that one can make a virtual correlation between the degree to which the clergy are professionalized and the degree to which they will have forfeited, or deliberately abandoned, their fundamental task of being brokers of truth." The church demonstrates its accommodation to modernity when it attempts to "market" itself to the culture around it. The marketing mentality causes the church to embrace modernity's penchant for commercialization and its therapeutic focus on the self. For Wells, "marketing the church" is a natural conclusion to evangelicals pushing theology out of the center in favor of reaching the modern consumer. As a result, Wells says, contemporary evangelicalism bears a closer resemblance to nineteenth-century liberalism than it does to its biblical foundations. The marketing concept is endemic to the seeker model. Wells, therefore, targets the seeker church for attack. In particular, he challenges Hybels with charges of milking the Christian faith of its truth content in the effort to reach a culture that is inherently predisposed to reject the gospel message.

### Wells' Assessment of Postmodernity

Wells assails evangelicals' apparent accommodation to modernity, but what of postmodernity? He assesses the claim that the West has entered a post-modern period. He criticizes the conventional use of this term, especially in academics. He believes that the use of the term by intellectuals in self-description is inaccurate because it derives from the modern mind's need to be better, new, and improved, as it reaches therapeutically toward the optimistic. He writes that it is, in fact, this assumption of an ability to move from one plateau of achievement to another that has given us a need always to be post: we feel compelled to assure ourselves that we are post-Puritan, post-Christian, and post-modern... [But] do we really think that as modern people we can extract ourselves from modernity and, in the next stage of the story of our progress, become post-modern? Therefore, if by postmodernity one means that the West has traveled beyond modernity, he says postmodernity is a farce and is an ideology of the intellectual elite. Instead, what many intellectuals call "postmodernism" is simply antimodernism, a disillusionment with the old Enlightenment ideals. Therefore, what Wells regards as a false use of the concept of postmodernity is the usage favored by intellectual elites. He acknowledges, however, the legitimacy of a cultural postmodernism. Western culture, he believes, has undergone significant shifts that have, rather than developed an alternative belief system, provided it with no belief system at all. "It is more like a vacuum into the quiet emptiness of which the self is reaching for meaning—and finding only itself." Postmodernism, therefore, is not the loss of the modernistic mentality, leaving it behind for something better. Instead, it is a cultural expression of the West's natural progression of modernism toward its own destruction. He explains that with the collapse of the Enlightenment project, postmodernity began to emerge in the 1970s. This collapse is manifested in the recognition by postmodernists that the promise of salvation in humanistic terms is completely fallacious, and in the despair that has ensued "post-modernists are now attacking...all beliefs in overarching meaning, all beliefs rooted in transcendent order, all values." The result is a new modernism in which the Christian faith, and all faiths, are made less plausible. In such a world, Wells rightly fears, churches led by pastors with anemic theology, pastors who have acquiesced to culture, will find that they can offer the world nothing that it does not already have in its society void of substance and truth. In a tone nearly pastoral in its despair, Wells says, "I want the church to be an alternative to postmodern culture, not a mere echo of it." In order to accomplish this commission, the church must desist from the worldliness that characterizes the culture and, second, the church must be a force for a countercultural spirituality. He rightly declares that "the church has no future if it chooses from weakness not to speak in its own language, the language of truth and understanding, in the postmodern world."

### So How Can a Pastor Reach Postmodern People?

Wells is right on several points that postmodern evangelical pastors cannot afford to dismiss. In fact, although it is an unsettling admittance, Wells is correct in his most central observation: The pastor is responsible for the presence, growth, and quality of the theology that drives the actions of the congregation—and many evangelical pastors today are purveyors of shoddy theology. The pastor usually manifests the quality of his theology in two areas, his church growth leadership and his pulpit ministry. If the pastor is result-conscious more than Christ-conscious in these two areas, he will decentralize theology and prayerful reflection for the benefit of production. Second, Wells correctly assesses the therapeutic and commercialistic mentalities that drive post-modernists. If congregations pander to these mentalities to profit in numbers, they will lose all credibility and, therefore, all influence as messengers of the truth. Fourth, pastors would be wise to consider a point that Wells overlooks. Pastors must be careful to clarify an implication rarely addressed by church growth teachers who are enthusiastic about the seeker-targeted model. Most of these churches begin with a "seeker-targeted worship service." Strictly speaking, however, unbelievers cannot worship because they have not submitted to Christ in the initial act of worship-repentance—that leads to salvation. Second, people are saved because God is the seeker. Therefore, as believers worship together, God is present and draws unbelievers to faith. For this reason, this writer maintains a careful distinction between the seeker-targeted service and the seeker-sensitive service. The latter is more appropriate. Finally, Wells is correct in his simple observation that "the children who have grown up or are growing up in the post-modern world bear its mark." Yet, at this point Wells disappoints the pastor. Wells acknowledges that postmodern people have a distinct worldview. He chastises pastors for trying to reach postmodernists without giving enough attention to sound theology. Then he promises that God in the Wasteland will build the active initial phase necessary for the church to recover its lost center in theology. Wells has prepared the field, gathered the team, called the plays, and promised to lead the pack by carrying the ball himself—and he fumbles right here, at the line of scrimmage. Rather than constructing an answer, Wells simply languishes on the question. He never explains how a pastor may play sound theology into effective forms for reaching postmodern people. In fact, he leaves the pastor in a void, insulting the shepherd with the implication that if the pastor is successfully reaching people using postmodern forms, he has abandoned sound theology and capitulated to the whims of the culture. Here lies Wells' greatest weakness. Whereas he warns against drawing the conclusion that a church's theology is healthy because it is successfully reaching its community, he fails to mention that, on the other hand, a growing church does not necessarily preclude healthy theology if the objective of the church is to evangelize its community, it would be absurd to regard success at attaining that objective to be a signal that the pastor has succumbed to modernity! What is left open for suggestion, therefore, would be ways by which evangelical pastors may maintain a sound theological base while effectively reaching the postmodern culture around them. Enter the seeker-oriented model. This writer sees at least three ways that the seeker-oriented model appropriately utilizes postmodern forms to convey biblical truth. In so doing, seeker churches are helping postmodernists move from a life apart from Christ to a life with Christ. They take seriously the postmodern mindset while sustaining loyalties to evangelical theology.

### By Addressing Self-centeredness in the Move to Christ-centeredness

As the shift in culture occurs, postmodernity is awash in commercialism and the therapeutic focus on the self. The way of the cross appears repulsive to a society driven by the way of the consumer. Therapy seems to matter more than holiness. As Wells declares, "The psychologizing of faith is destroying the Christian mind." Indeed, nothing endangers Christian discipleship like self-worship. How can the evangelical church help postmodernists move away from this self-centeredness toward Christ-centeredness? In the postmodern church, recovery ministries are included as discipleship. The postmodern believer has rejected the atomism and fragmentation of the modernists and now searches for discipleship methods that account for the redemption and sanctification of the whole person. This, in fact, is a more biblical approach than those that view the self as fragmented, able to be saved, and then sanctified in separate steps or compartments. Evangelical churches in the postmodern world will take the new holism into account, seeing evangelism and discipleship as twins in one process. Seeker churches recognize that postmodernists, particularly baby-busters, have experienced brokenness and desire wholeness and healing. Nurtured with nourishment from the Bible, these methods can help believers grow into holistic, Christ-centered lives.

### By Stressing Community Over Individuality

Modernists hailed the autonomous self and stressed individuality over community. As a result, as Wells observes, postmodernists have inherited a culture in which ties to place, community, and family have been severed. Seeker-oriented churches are reaching postmodernists by stressing that the people of God are a cohesive, redemptive community. Postmodern people desire a place where they belong. Their dysfunctional families lacked the stability that builds trust and devotion. As denominational loyalty declines among postmodernists, their interest in small communities increases. Seeker-oriented churches reach people by lovingly assimilating them into the local body rather than institutionalizing their loyalties. Whereas evangelical churches have tended toward an emphasis on the individual commitment to Christ, in the postmodern context this should be coupled to the teaching that upon conversion the believer becomes a part of the local church community. The revival of the small group movement in the local church demonstrates this return to community. Larger churches in particular are finding the assimilation of young believers takes place far better within smaller units rather than in the larger local body.

### By Encouraging Participation Instead of Observation

The modern scientific worldview teaches that a whole can be broken down into its constituent parts, and each part may be observed and addressed without reference to the whole. For postmodernists, however, modernistic objectivity is a naive assumption. Churches that reach postmodern students advocate incarnation, contextulization, and participation. Against the epistemology of modernity which viewed the spectator as an unbiased observer of reality, postmodern people are predisposed to decry the emphasis on impartial observation that undergirded modernity's scientific worldview. They believe in the participation of the knower in his or her understanding of the world outside. In relation to the church, in worship and in ministry, they are not interested in being spectators but prefer hands-on ministry that proves the viability of their faith. Further, the postmodern lives in the heritage of Wittgenstein. The postmodernist does not receive disseminated truth without reference to his or her context. So he or she is not likely to listen to the traditional pastor, an orator who believes one has the right to be heard based on the authority of one's position. Knowing this, pastors of seeker churches are setting the pace for the twenty-first century by developing a conversational style of teaching God's word with the focus on application to the life of the believer. They are teachers and communicators. Rather than expecting a hearing because of their position, they gain credibility by their lifestyle and convey that they are also learning from their personal journey with Christ. Similarly, postmodernists are relativists. Modernists taught them that the self has all authority. Whereas this belief still guides much of their thinking, they are discontent with secularism and have turned toward a new spirituality. They are more open than their parents to learn truth, but with a definitive difference: They regard it as their right to decide what is true, and they base this decision on function, not external authority. Therefore, the postmodern church will utilize hands-on ministry as an avenue for teaching absolute truth. Baby-busters have no interest in formulaic doctrines or creeds. For the vital doctrines of the church to continue, evangelical pastors must allow their postmodernists to wrestle with the scriptural truths from which the doctrines are derived, to live out and learn those truths, and with Scripture as the criterion for truth, weave their doctrines out of the fabric of real life.

### Conclusion

By no means do all methods work in all the same contexts. Nevertheless, while the local pastor must respect the challenge to be a broker of truth to his or her generation, church growth methodologists correctly teach that a changing Western culture requires new methods for reaching twenty-first-century people. All pastors should heed the advice of one pastor of a growing, seeker-oriented congregation. "Our goal," James White teaches, "is translation—not transformation—of the message." Robert A. Weathers is the pastor of Meadow Ridge Church in Maiden, NC. He holds the Ph.D. from Southwestern Seminary and also serves as an adjunct professor of religion and philosophy at the Catawba campus of Gardner-Webb University.

## 3. Thematic Analysis Matrices
### Theme: The Seeker-Targeted Paradigm
The document defines this as a methodology that seeks to present the gospel in forms understandable to the unchurched. It is presented as an application of the missionary principle of integrating form and content, specifically targeting those who are unreachable by traditional church methods.

### Theme: The Critique of Modernity and Postmodernity
The author discusses the shift from the Enlightenment-based modernist worldview to a postmodern one. David Wells is cited as a critic who argues that the church has lost its theological center by adopting the commercial and therapeutic values of modernity, leading to a professionalized clergy that has abandoned its role as 'brokers of truth'.

### Theme: The Role of the Pastor
The text emphasizes that the pastor is responsible for the quality of theology in the church. It argues that pastors must move away from being mere orators to becoming conversational teachers who facilitate participation and hands-on ministry, allowing believers to engage with scripture in the context of their real lives.

### Theme: Community and Participation
The document identifies a shift away from the modernist emphasis on the autonomous individual toward a postmodern desire for community and belonging. It suggests that seeker-oriented churches effectively reach postmodernists by fostering small groups and redemptive communities rather than relying on individualistic models of discipleship.
