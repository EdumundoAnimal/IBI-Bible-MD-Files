## 1. Verbatim Source Text
### Introduction to Hermeneutics

Review - Intro to Hermeneutics: Definition of, Need for, Challenges to, Approach for class. Definition: Hermeneutics: 'The art and science of interpretation'. Greek hermeneia: 'interpretation, translation'. Describes the principles used to understand what something means, to understand what a message intends to communicate. Need: Every reader is an interpreter anyway. The tension between eternal significance and historical particularity. Obstacles: Distance of time. Distance of culture. Distance of geography. Distance of language.

### OT Interpretation

OT Period, Intertestamental period, NT Period, Patristic period, Medieval period, Reformation period, Enlightenment period, Modern period, Postmodern. Are there instances where the Bible interprets itself (particularly in the OT)? If so, can you think of any examples? Old Testament: there are hints of interpretation within the OT itself, as one passage may use or clarify something written earlier. 1-2 Chronicles as post-exilic reinterpretation of 1-2 Kings. Isaiah 31:1 - reference to exodus. Old Testament: the first time we hear of actual need for interpretation is after the exile. Cf. Neh. 8:7-8 where Ezra reads law of Moses and Levites EXPLAIN what he was reading to crowd: The Levites ... instructed the people in the Law while the people were standing there. They read from the Book of the Law of God, making it clear and giving the meaning so that the people could understand what was being read. Need for interpretation: What factors in the biblical period might have brought about the need to interpret the Bible? Need for interpretation: Loss of language - Hebrew -> Aramaic (exile factor). Ambiguity of text (punctuation, etc). Cultural change: ideas, institutions, politics, etc. over time. The same distances that we encounter today were encountered then...

### Intertestamental Period

Intertestamental period: commentaries. Intertestamental Judaism also gave rise to pseudapigraphal and apocryphal books which interpreted biblical stories and laws. These were in many ways the first biblical commentaries. 1-4 Macabees, Jubilees. Book of Jubilees (250 BCE) on Genesis 11:27-28: And the child (Abram) began to understand the errors of the earth that all went astray after graven images and after uncleanness, and his father taught him writing, and he was two weeks of years old (14), and he separated himself from his father that he might not worship idols with him. And he began to pray to the creator of all things... Intertestamental period: Hellenistic Judaism. Hellenistic Judaism: Influenced by the Greek empire which ruled at this time (333 BCE and on). Tried to integrate Greek (Platonic) philosophy with Jewish religion. Wanted his faith and Scripture to make sense to Greek friends and colleagues. Major distinctive was allegorical method. Text serves as a kind of extended metaphor which points to hidden ideas beyond it. Best seen in Alexandrian Jew Philo. Philo (20 BCE-54CE): Believed the text had both a literal and an allegorical meaning. Spent a lot of time on numbers and names in bible to see what they 'really' meant. Philo on creation... III. (13) And he says that the world was made in six days, not because the Creator stood in need of a length of time (for it is natural that God should do everything at once, not merely by uttering a command, but by even thinking of it); but because the things created required arrangement; and number is akin to arrangement; and, of all numbers, six is, by the laws of nature, the most productive: for of all the numbers, from the unit upwards, it is the first perfect one, being made equal to its parts, and being made complete by them; the number three being half of it, and the number two a third of it, and the unit a sixth of it, and, so to say, it is formed so as to be both male and female, and is made up of the power of both natures;

### NT Interpretation

NT Interpretation (apostolic period - 30-100 CE). Out of this complex Jewish matrix comes Christian interpretation within the NT. All of the apostles and almost all of the NT writers were Jewish and thus were influenced by Jewish culture and the various intertestamental interpretations we've just covered. What are some of the ways that the NT writers use or interpret the OT? They often appealed to Israel's scriptures (OT) to back up their claims: Jesus was the promised Messiah. The community following after him were Judaism's true hope. Jesus himself used this idea of literal fulfillment with regard to himself: Luke 4:18-21 = Is. 61:1-2. Luke 7:21-23 = Is. 35:5-6. Paul uses this same methodology: 2 Cor. 3:14-16 = Ex. 34:33-35. This is called a 'Christological' reading of the OT - reading it in the light of Christ. Other interpretive approaches in NT: Typology: divinely inspired 'types' (patterns or symbols) that anticipate or predict God's activity later in history. Matt. 2:17 = Jer. 31:15 - What once related to the exile now relates to the death of young boys.

### Patristic Period

Patristic Period: apostolic fathers. Apostolic Fathers (100-150 CE). Our sources come from church leaders such as Clement (right), Ignatius, Polycarp, writing mostly to new Christian churches; these were the 'disciples of the disciples'. Traditional interpretation: for the first time in Christian interpretation we see an interpretation held because it has apostolic succession - this was to fight a growing number of heresies. i.e. a disciple passed it down to his disciple, who told me, so it's right. Continue to employ methods given before them: Literal-contextual meaning. Typological meaning (patterns or symbols). A new approach which becomes very popular, common in Hellenistic thought (e.g. Philo): allegory. Typology & allegory (more on this in week 9): Allegory sees the hidden, spiritual meaning of a text. Typology see the revelatory connection (symbols) between two historically distinct but religiously significant persons or events. Allegorical interpretation: True reality lies behind what the words actually say. The literal meaning of the words don't carry the full meaning. Find a 'spiritual' meaning behind the words. Augustine: The four rivers in Genesis 2:10-14 = four cardinal virtues (prudence, justice, temperance, fortitude). In the Fall: the fig leaves = hypocrisy, the skin covering = mortality. Patristic Period: Alexandria and Antioch. Antioch: Became a centre for those who thought the Alexandrians went too far w/ allegory. Taught the literal-contextual understanding of scripture - every passage has one, plain meaning. Theodore of Mopsuestia; Theodore; John Chysostom. This struggle - between a more literal approach and a more allegorical approach - would continue for over a thousand years, right down to the Reformation and beyond... Again, we see here the struggle between eternal significance and historical particularity. Patristic Period: Church Councils. Church Councils (400-590 CE). Who was the Emperor Constantine, and what significance did he have on the history of Christianity? As Constantine 'Christianised' the empire, he wanted theological unity. Councils became the means whereby this happened. Appeals to scripture alone led to stalemate - if there was a disagreement, both sides thought they were being biblical! cf. Alexandria and Antioch. Led to greater dependence on tradition and apostolic succession because they couldn't agree. Augustine: one must find out what original writer intended to say; if impossible: Consult the 'rule of faith' - what other passages of scripture say; Consult the authority of the church, or traditional interpretation; Consult context - plainer passages take precedent of obscurer passages.

### Middle Ages/Medieval Period

Middle Ages/Medieval Period. Eventually a proponent of the literal interpretation would form: scholasticism. Scholasticism was a pre-Renaissance intellectual awakening that started in the monasteries. Concerned with relationship between faith and reason. Thomas Aquinas: With Aquinas the allegorical method began to decline. Use of reason and history became important. forerunner of the Enlightenment and scientific revolution. Middle Ages/medieval period: review. Solidification of tradition as important part of interpretation. Allegory expands into 4 senses of Scripture. Later middle ages sees rise of scholasticism, which is the beginning of a return to literal interpretation (Aquinas). History of Interpretation: review... Interpretation within the OT. Intertestamental period. NT Period. Patristic period. Medieval period. OT Interpretation: review. Later OT books draw on previous writings (Chronicles, Isaiah, etc). Toward the end of the OT era, people need help understanding Scripture because of language and cultural issues (Ezra & Nehemiah). Thus - the interpretive process has begun... Intertestamental period: review. Translations appear - they make interpretive decisions (eg. Targum). First commentaries: people begin to retell biblical stories and embellish them to make heroes look better, enemies look worse (eg. Jubilees). Some begin to interpret Scripture apologetically, so that it makes sense to others outside the faith (eg. Philo). People begin to see events from their own time when reading Scripture (eg. Qumran).

## 3. Thematic Analysis Matrices
### Theme: Hermeneutics
Defined as the art and science of interpretation, focusing on the principles used to understand the intended communication of a message.

### Theme: Tension of Interpretation
The document highlights the ongoing struggle between maintaining the eternal significance of scripture while acknowledging its historical particularity.

### Theme: Allegorical vs. Literal Interpretation
A recurring theme throughout the history of interpretation, contrasting the search for hidden spiritual meanings (allegory) with the focus on the plain, literal-contextual meaning of the text.

### Theme: Development of Tradition
The document notes that as disagreements arose, there was a shift toward relying on church authority, tradition, and apostolic succession to resolve interpretive stalemates.
