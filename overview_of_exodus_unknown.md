# Overview of Exodus
**Author:** [[Unknown]]

## 1. Verbatim Source Text
### Introduction

The story that began in Genesis continues in the book of Exodus. In the Hebrew Exodus begins with ‘and these’ directly indicating continuity. This is not translated this way by most English bibles (The NASB uses “now these”). In many ways it parallels the gospel of Luke followed by the book of Acts. In Genesis, God chose Abraham promising through him a great nation from which all nations of the earth will be blessed (Gen 12:1-3) and in Exodus the story continues with the completion of the promise through the formation of the nation of Israel. At the end of Genesis Abraham’s offspring had just moved to Egypt. There where only seventy in total (Gen 46:27). Exodus begins by reminding us that these seventy where all there was at the beginning but that in the succeeding four hundred years they had grown fruitful and had multiplied and were now very strong, filling the entire land. As a result, they became greatly oppressed which only served to increase their numbers. God’s providence was over them despite the Pharaohs’ attempts of controlling the population (Ex 1:1-21). And so, begins the story of the great lengths to which God determined to create a people for himself, a “kingdom of priests and a holy nation” (Ex 19:6). It is through this nation that God’s promised universal blessing to Abraham would be accomplished (P.E. Enns, The Book of Exodus, In New Dictionary of Biblical Theology, Downer Grove, Illinois: Intervarsity Press, 2000, 146.). Exodus tells the story of how God accomplished the second part of his plan. The God who chose Abraham, Isaac, and then Jacob rescued his people out of Egypt not because of their merit or righteousness but because he had made a promise to their forefathers (Gen 3:16; 4:5). God had not forgotten them. Their growth into a mighty people, their oppression and now their rescue and deliverance was always the way God had planed it (“I have watched over you” 3:16). The time had now come for it to be realized.

### Outline

There are many outlines possible for Exodus but perhaps a simple one is to divide Exodus into three parts (Peter Enns, Exodus, The NIC Application Commentary, Grand Rapids:Michigan, 2000, 33-36): I. Departure from Egypt (chapters 1 through 15): This includes the historical setting (chapter 1), the birth, rescue and escape of Moses (chapter 2), the call of Moses (chapter 2-3), increased suffering (4:1-7:13), the plagues (7:14-11:10), the Passover and departure (chapter 12 to 13:16), and the crossing of the Red Sea (13:17-15:21). II. Mount Sinai and the Law (chapter 16 to 24): Right after the crossing of the Red Sea the Israelites travel into the desert where they run out of water (15:22-27), they then complain about the food and God provides their daily them manna and quail (chapters 16 and 17). They again run out of water (17:1-7) and are attacked for the first time by the Amalekites (17:8-16). Moses sets up a government structure (chapter 18) and finally after three months they arrive at Mount Sinai. Here Moses is given the Ten Commandments (chapter 20) which is followed by the Book of the Covenant (chapters 21-23) and the covenant is confirmed (chapter 24). III. Mount Sinai and the Tabernacle (chapter 25 to 40): The people are given instructions for the building and furnishing of the tabernacle (chapters 25 to 40:33). However right in the middle of these instructions there is the terrible rebellion of the people where Aaron creates the golden calf, which is followed by punishment, repentance, intercession and forgiveness resulting in the new set of stone tablets (chapter 32 to 34). Finally, at the end of the book the Glory of the Lord fills the Tabernacle (40:34-38).

### Biblical Themes

There are many grand biblical themes that are developed in Exodus. This is so much so that understanding Exodus is foundational to understand the biblical themes of the rest of the Bible. The key passage in Exodus is Exodus 5:22-6:8 where God tells the Israelites who he is (notice the 4 “I am the LORD”), what he is doing (notice the 7 “I will”), and why he is doing it (the sworn promise to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob). God’s design and plan as depicted in this text is pivotal to understanding God’s purpose in the all of the Old Testament (Elmer A. Martens, God’s Design: A Focus on Old Testament Theology, 3d. ed. N. Richland Hills, Tex.:Bibal, 1998). There are four main themes of God’s revelation of himself to his people; these are: deliverance and salvation, community, knowledge of God, and land. 1. Deliverance and salvation: God’s purpose of deliverance and salvation are depicted as physical events through God acting as a warrior, and spiritually by providing the Law (6:6) through covenant (20-24). This theme of deliverance is not however from slavery to personal independence; it is from bad bondage and worship to good bondage and worship (4:23; 7:16; 8:1,20; 9:1,13; 10:3). Freedom from bondage was accomplished entirely by God. The people are described as helpless and weak before the threats and power of the Pharaoh. It is only God who can deliver them. The deliverance in Exodus is a foreshadowing of the deliverance in the New Covenant where God overthrows bondage to sin and Satan. As in Exodus, people are helpless without God’s saving intervention. And as well, it is a change of masters: from sin to God (Rom 6:11,13,17,20-22; 7:25). (Douglas K. Stuart, Exodus: An exegetical and Theological Exposition of Holy Scripture, The New American Commentary Volume 2, Nashville, Tennessee: Broadman & Holman Publishers, 2006). 2. Covenant Community: God’s purpose to form a godly community is found in Exodus 6:7a where he states that “I will take you as my own people, and I will be your God.” Right from the beginning of Exodus, when God speaks to Pharaoh he refers to the Israelites as “my people” (5:1; 7:16; 8:1,20-21; 9:1,13; 10:3). God views the Israelites as his own unique people, different from all other people of earth (19:6). He showed this by delivering and saving them from the greatest evil power on earth and establishing a covenant relationship with them (chs. 19-24). This covenant defines a personal relationship between God and his people and outlines how the people, as his special representatives should relate to each other. (Stuart, Exodus, 38). 3. Knowledge of God: God’s purpose to reveal himself as “the LORD their God” is found in Exodus 6:7-8: “Then you will know that I am the LORD your God, who brought you out from under the yoke of the Egyptians ... I am the LORD.” Martens states in God’s Design: “This means, among other things, that he offers himself to be known. God invites his people into the adventure of knowing him.” (Martens, Exodus, 13). Such knowledge is not information about the characteristics of God but reflects the covenant relationship that reveals the nature of who God is. The ability for the people to know God through his covenant relationship was not given because of their adherence to the Law (just look at chapters 32-33!) but out of divine grace. Throughout Exodus, knowledge and relationship with God is done primarily through God’s representative, Moses. 4. Land: God’s intent is to give his people the land for their possession (6:8): “I will bring you to the land I swore with uplifted hand to give to Abraham, to Isaac and to Jacob. I will give it to you as a possession. I am the Lord.” This land is more than simple geography; rather it represents where God’s people reside, where they worship him and where he blesses them. To leave the land is, in some sense, to leave his blessings. To come into the land, even as a foreigner, and to be under God’s covenant is to be brought into his blessings. Land is a difficult theme for Christians since there is so little reference to land in the New Testament. What land represents in the Old Testament is represented differently in the New Testament. Instead of receiving land, the New Testament people of God are to live in the Kingdom of God. Geographically the Kingdom of God may be anywhere on earth, but theologically it is similar to the Old Testament concept; it is where we live in covenant relationship with God, under his authority and in his blessings. There are many other themes we could explore but this is all the time we have for now. Exodus and the New Testament: There are many references and allusions to Exodus in the New Testament. For a great survey see the article by Rick Watts in New Dictionary of Biblical Theology (R.E. Watts, Exodus, in New Dictionary of Biblical Theology, 484-487).

### Moses and Jesus

In Hebrews 3:1-4:11, the writer compares Moses to Jesus and explains that Jesus is a better Moses. He also states that Jesus is a better high priest and a better tabernacle. Enns states this well as follows (Enns, Book of Exodus, 150-152): This new and better Moses has come to bring his people, the church, the new Israel, out of a slavery far worse that that imposed by Egypt – a slavery to sin and death. And by his death and resurrection he has defeated an anti-God figure much more heinous than a mere human pharaoh – Satan himself. And, having himself proceeded to a new heavenly country, he will one day return to bring his people to a land far better than Israel’s piece of land in the Middle East – heaven itself. In the meantime the church, having experienced its own Exodus from sin and death, is now in its desert march awaiting that final redemption (Heb. 3:7-4:11). ## 3. Thematic Analysis Matrices
### Theme: Deliverance and Salvation
Exodus depicts God as a warrior who delivers his people from physical bondage. This is a foreshadowing of the New Covenant where God delivers humanity from the bondage of sin and Satan, requiring a change of masters from sin to God.

### Theme: Covenant Community
God forms a community by establishing a covenant, defining a personal relationship where he is their God and they are his people. This relationship sets them apart as unique representatives of God on earth.

### Theme: Knowledge of God
Knowledge of God in Exodus is not merely intellectual information but a relational experience of God's nature through his covenant, granted by divine grace rather than adherence to the Law.

### Theme: Land
The land represents the place of God's presence, worship, and blessing. In the New Testament, this concept shifts from a geographical location to the Kingdom of God, where believers live under God's authority.
