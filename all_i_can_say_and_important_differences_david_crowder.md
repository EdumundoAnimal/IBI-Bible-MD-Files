### Important Differences

1. Canaanite (Matthew) v. Syrophoenician (Mark)
Canaanites were the old (non-existent) enemies of Israel. This would not be implicit in the term Syrophoenician (Syrian Phoenicia). Matthew may be trying to use as derogatory a term as possible so the impact of the exorcism is greater. Jesus heals even the old hated enemies of Israel.

2. Son of David
This term is used by the woman in Matthew. It is a Jewish term which would be understood by Jews as a messianic designation. It is also a huge leap of faith for a Gentile to make. It is interesting that Matthew, writing to the Jews to demonstrate the coming of Messiah in fulfilment of prophecy, uses this term. Jesus also ministers to what the Jews would see as the worst of people.

3. The woman kneels
This implies worship. Matthew may be trying to emphasise the fact that a Gentile has accepted Jesus for Who He is. She also calls Jesus 'Lord'. This may not be that significant.

4. The woman is condemned more in Matthew than in Mark
Again, this may relate to Matthew making a point about the Jews rejecting and a Gentile accepting. Despite being rebutted, she perseveres. Shows her total acceptance and faith?

The disciples' remarks could actually be positive here - they could be asking Jesus to send her away by healing her.

5. Jesus commends her faith
Matthew could be contrasting her with the Pharisees.

- How does this story in these two versions illustrate the principle of reading the gospels horizontally and vertically?

## 3. Thematic Analysis Matrices
### Theme: Horizontal and Vertical Gospel Reading
The document explores the differences between the accounts of the Syrophoenician woman in Matthew and Mark to illustrate the importance of reading the gospels both horizontally (comparing the accounts side-by-side) and vertically (understanding the specific theological intent of each individual author).

### Theme: Theological Intent of Matthew
The text argues that Matthew uses specific terminology like 'Canaanite' and 'Son of David' to emphasize the universal reach of Jesus' ministry, even to the 'hated enemies' of Israel, while simultaneously highlighting the contrast between Gentile faith and Jewish rejection.
