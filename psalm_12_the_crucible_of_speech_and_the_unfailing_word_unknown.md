### 1. Linguistic & Syntax Report

Textual Variants The primary textual witnesses for Psalm 12 comprise the Masoretic Text (MT, Codex Leningradensis B19A), the Septuagint ($G$/LXX, Codex Vaticanus and Sinaiticus), the Dead Sea Scrolls fragments (principally 4QPs$^a$ [4Q83] and 11QPs$^a$), the Syriac Peshitta ($S$), and the Aramaic Targum ($T$). Verse 7 (MT v. 8) — The Pronominal Suffix Puzzle: The MT exhibits an alternation of third-person object suffixes: tišmərēm, "You will keep them" — 3mp) in parallel with tiṣṣərennû, "You will preserve him/it" — 3ms with energic nun). The Septuagint ($G$), Peshitta ($S$), and Vulgate harmonize both verbs to the first-person plural: tišmərēnû ("You will keep us") and tiṣṣərēnû ("You will preserve us"). The internal evidence strongly supports the MT as the lectio difficilior. The shifting antecedents reflect Davidic collective singulars: the third masculine plural (-ēm) refers back to the ‘ănîyyîm and ’ebyônîm ("the afflicted" and "the needy") of verse 5, while the third masculine singular (-ennû) points synthetically either to the singular faithful one (ḥāsîd) of verse 1 or to the individual who pants for deliverance (yāpîaḥ lô) in verse 5. Verse 6 (MT v. 7) — The Crux of ba‘ălîl lā’āreṣ: The MT reads kesep ṣārûp ba‘ălîl lā’āreṣ). The noun ’ălîl) is a biblical hapax legomenon. Classical rabbinic lexicography (Rashi, Ibn Ezra) linked it to an earthen crucible (crucible on/in the earth). Modern comparative Semitic philology (WBC, HALOT) correlates the root with Ugaritic ‘ll (to go over a second time, rework, refine). The prepositional phrase lā’āreṣ is an adverbial accusative or locative expressing spatial separation (separated from the earth/dross or in an earthen crucible). The textual coherence remains intact without emendation. Verse 8 (MT v. 9) — The Syntactic Obscurity of kərum zullût: The MT phrase kərum zullût libnê ’ādām) features the preposition kə prefixed to the infinitive construct rûm, "to be high, exalted") governing the abstract noun zullût, derived from the root zll, "to be light, worthless, vile"). The LXX translators wrestled with this phrase, translating kata to hypsos sou epolyōrēsas, "according to your height you multiplied"), mistakenly reading the consonantal framework as a divine address. The MT reading represents the preserved authentic text, offering an ironic, somber climax: the circular prowling of the wicked is directly tied to the sociological phenomenon wherein human society exalts moral degeneracy. Grammatical Mechanics The poetic syntax of Psalm 12 moves between imperative supplication, indicative lament, jussive imprecation, direct divine oracular speech, and participial affirmation.

## 3. Thematic Analysis Matrices
### Theme: Covenant Fidelity
The document emphasizes that the faithful (ḥāsîd) are defined by their vertical devotion and societal relationships anchored in Yahweh's covenant love (ḥesed). The disappearance of these individuals in Psalm 12:1 signals a total disintegration of relational solidarity.

### Theme: Epistemic Integrity
The text argues that language is the foundation of society. When language is weaponized through flattery (śəpat ḥălāqôt) and double-dealing (bəlēb wālēb), truth is discarded, leading to the systemic exploitation of the vulnerable.

### Theme: Divine Preservation
Despite the unresolved tension of the wicked prowling on every side, the righteous are secure because the Word of God ('imrôt YHWH) provides an unshakable existential platform, tested and refined like silver seven times over.
