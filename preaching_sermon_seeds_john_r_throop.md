### Preaching: Sermon Seeds Commentary

No matter our stage in life, we always are on the verge of human frailty. In the end, we are dust. That is a common human condition. Psalm 90:3 says, 'Turn back, you mortals.' In On Death and Dying (New York: Scribner, 2003), Elisabeth Kübler-Ross reflects on the natural process of the end of life. We all shall die, and we all go through a death and dying process. 'We may achieve peace - our own inner peace as well as peace between nations - by facing and accepting the reality of our own death' (p. 30). God does not change. Our own understanding may change from previous generations; in other ways, there's nothing new. As we read, 'For a thousand years in your sight are like yesterday when it is past, or like a watch in the night' (Ps 90:4). Theologian J. I. Packer said that, in the perplexities of life, we understand the truth that the ancients knew. In the urgency of the nuclear age, he said: 'God and his Christ remain the same - almighty to save.' (Knowing God, Downers Grove, IL: InterVarsity Press, 1983, p. 72). And the grave is not the goal: Dust thou art, to dust returnest, Was not spoken of the soul.' Longfellow, one of America's beloved 19th-century poets, deals with death's certainty and its romantic glory. ### Children's Time: Forever

Bring a jump rope and a ball. Ask the children to say how they are different. Accept all answers; then point out that a jump rope has two ends, so it has a beginning and an end. The ball is a circle and has no beginning and no end. Spin the ball to illustrate. Mention that in the Psalm 90 we hear that God has no beginning and no end. God has always been and always will be. Asking the children to join in saying 'forever and ever,' tell how God will be God forever and ever, and God's love for us lasts forever and ever. Comment that one of the neat things about a ball is that we can share it with others. We can also share the good news about God's love. Gather the children and have a whisper practice first. Face the congregation and shout the good news, 'God's love lasts forever!' Pray with the children, giving thanks for God's never-ending love.

### Focus: Psalm 46 (The Values of Refuge and Strength)

For sermon materials based on Romans 3:19-28, see Minister's Annual Manual 2008-2009; for sermon materials based on John 8:31-36, see the July/August 2008 issue of The Clergy Journal. This text is known as a 'Jerusalem celebration' psalm. Few other psalms are focused on the center of government and worship: Psalm 47 (God's victorious reign) and Psalm 48 (the royal city of God's earthly kingdom). These three psalms, most likely pre-exilic, were part of the liturgical celebration of Jewish pilgrimage to Jerusalem, and were sung in the temple. Psalm 46 is constructed in a classic hymn format. There are three verses. Most commentators believe that the first section (vv. 1-3) was sung by citizens of Jerusalem and pilgrims to the temple. The Levitical choir most likely sang the second stanza (vv 4-7), and the third stanza (vv 8-11) was sung as people moved in procession through the Upper Gate and followed the Levitical choir through an inner courtyard toward the Gate of Song. In verse 4, the psalm notes that 'there is a river whose streams make glad the city of God.' There was no stream or other active body of water in the temple. Instead, the text is a metaphor for the continual outpouring of the sustaining and refreshing blessings of God, which make the temple city Jerusalem like the Garden of Eden. The psalm also reflects a time of relative peace which lasted during the later reign of David and Solomon's entire rule (approximately 60 years). Jews of the time certainly appreciated verse 9: God 'makes wars cease to the end of the earth' and 'he breaks the bow, and shatters the spear [and] burns the shields with fire.' Through Jewish history, warfare and instability were a regular problem, as in nearly every area of the Middle East - and still are today. The word selah appears 71 times in the psalms (three times in this psalm). There is no specific meaning for the term. It may have signified a pause in the vocal music while an instrumental interlude played in the liturgical procession to move participants from one location to another. - John R. Throop

## 3. Thematic Analysis Matrices
### Theme: Human Frailty and Transience
The text emphasizes the dichotomy between eternal divine existence and the transitory nature of human life, citing Psalm 90 to illustrate that humans are 'dust' and that life is fragile regardless of status.

### Theme: Divine Immutability
The author argues that while human understanding evolves, God remains constant, citing J.I. Packer to emphasize that God and Christ remain 'almighty to save' amidst the perplexities of life.

### Theme: Liturgical Celebration
The analysis of Psalm 46 identifies it as a 'Jerusalem celebration' psalm, detailing its historical use in temple processions and its function as a hymn of refuge and strength.
