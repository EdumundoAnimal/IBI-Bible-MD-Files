# Profitable and Unprofitable Shepherds: Economic and Theological Perspectives on Ezekiel 34
**Author:** [[Andrew Mein]]

## 1. Cleaned Document Text
The article examines Ezekiel 34, specifically the "good shepherd" metaphor in light of the economics of animal husbandry in the Ancient Near East. It argues that while the text contains theological rhetoric regarding restoration, it is deeply rooted in the practical socio-economic realities of sheep farming. Mein explores how the prophetic critique of leaders (the "unprofitable shepherds") is constructed by juxtaposing their neglect with the expected duties of an owner-shepherd. By analyzing historical evidence from contracts and legal texts, the author demonstrates that the metaphor reflects an awareness of the contractual responsibilities inherent in managing another's property. The article concludes that the restoration of Israel described in Ezekiel 34 functions not just as a religious hope but as a return to a proper order where YHWH, as the true owner, restores the flock, thereby reclaiming what was misappropriated by ineffective leaders.

## 3. Thematic Analysis Matrices
### Theme: Economic Metaphor
The use of sheep farming and pastoral management as a metaphor for divine leadership and socio-political responsibility in Ancient Israel.

### Theme: Contractual Obligation
The argument that Ezekiel 34 utilizes language derived from real-world animal husbandry contracts to judge the failure of Israel's leaders.

### Theme: Theology of Restoration
The assertion that YHWH's restoration of the people is fundamentally about reclaiming property that was abused by self-interested 'unprofitable' shepherds.
