# The Influence of the Post Nicene Fathers
**Author:** [[Unknown]]

## 1. Cleaned Document Text
The Post Nicene Fathers were influential in the ways of commentaries, interpretation, translation, preservation of original texts, and involvement in the affairs of state. The Post Nicene Fathers can be broken into two groups, the Eastern grammatical/historical school of interpretation and the Western Allegorical school of interpretation. John Chrysostom/East: John was posthumously known as Chrysostom, due to his brilliant speaking ability. Was ascetic in nature and spent many years as a monk, but eventually became patriarch over Constantinople. Spoke against outrageous display of wealth, when Empress Eudoxia displayed a silver statue of herself next to St. Sophia. Not only an excellent speaker but well versed in biblical interpretation. Although he did not know how to read Hebrew, he sought to find the literal meaning in the OT and made some wonderful commentaries. Although he lacked tact, he always emphasised the importance of morals paired with religion; the two must go in hand. This came across in his speaking. John Chrysostom is still regarded as the greatest orator the East had. Theodore: Theodore was considered the prince of ancient exegesis. He opposed the allegorical system of interpretation and insisted on understanding the grammar and history of the text to discover the meaning in the text. Theodore was also most precise in his interpretations of even some of the most obscure or remote texts, helping better understand a text as a whole. This detailed study, learning under the tutelage of Diodorus of Tarsus, made him an excellent commentator and theologian. He also wrote and excellent commentary on the books of Thesallonians and Collosians. Both him and John Chrysostom were a marked contrast from the strained allegorical interpretations of that time. Eusebius: Eusebius was called the Father of Church History, and with good reason. His greatest work, Ecclesiastical History, was the history of the church from the apostolic times up until 324. His purpose in its work was to see the troubled times that marked it and how it transitioned into a period of prosperity. Due to his gentle nature and dislike of quarrelling, he made the perfect right hand to Constantine the Great during the Council of Nicaea. He helped in devising and writing up the Nicaean Creed, hoping to avoid argument between the Greek East, Latin West, and the troublesome Arians of that time. Eusebius had an incredible thirst for knowledge, both profane and scriptural. Eusebius amassed a library of no small measure, helping to preserve many texts. Also, his vast knowledge of these text helped preserve some of these sources in his own works, by quoting or paraphrasing. Although some of his work was marred by a lack of footnoting, he is especially noted for his scientific attention to detail in reliable sources. He made sure that he had the most trustworthy and reliable sources available to him. Jerome/West: Jerome was a wandering student in Rome and the cities of Gaul. Taking up the monastic life, he began to learn Hebrew from a converted Jew. Having a knowledge of Greek and of Hebrew, he set about translating one of the most important works of his time, the Latin Vulgate. The Vulgate is the books of the Old and New Testament in Latin, a language common in the Western culture of that time. The Vulgate eventually became the official texts of the Bible for the Catholic church, and remained so only until recently. Jerome was also an able commentator. Due to his love of the classics, philosophy came through in some of his works. Jerome was passionate about the ascetic life, and wrote many works were based on the monastic life. Ambrose: Ambrose was known as a great administrator of church, an excellent orator, and one of the first to include singing of hymns and psalmody into the church. He was an incredible Theologian, despite coming from a governmental background. Ambrose took up the position of Bishop of Milan in 374, and went about studying theology. He spoke against the Arian groups, unafraid to pick a side in the matter. Ambrose even showed courage in 390 when he refused admission to the Lords supper to the Emperor Theodosius. During that time, Theodosius ordered the massacre of 7000 people after the governor of that city had been murdered. Ambrose insisted that Theodosius repent of his sin publicly. Ambrose wanted to make sure the rulers of the state respected the church and did not transgress the claims of the church in spiritual matters. Ambrose is also known as personally influencing Augustine of Hippo, which later led to his conversion. Augustine often criticised the biblical texts for its rhetoric style. Ambrose, being a gifted speaker, influenced Augustine's view of Christianity. Augustine: Augustine of Hippo is considered to be the most influential of the Post Nicene Fathers. Augustine wrote a vast amount of works, many of which have influenced church today. The Reformers would see him as an equal to men such as Luther and Calvin. The Catholics would see his works as the foundational bulwark to many of their doctrines. The Roman Catholics consider him one of the greatest doctors of the religion. Many of Augustine's work are often filtered through manicheanist, neo-platonic thinking. Prior to his conversion in 386, Augustine was a follower of Mani, and later began studies in philosophy. The classics often show up in his work. His understanding of these two schools of thought later helped him convert many Manicheanists in North Africa, where the religion was popular. Augustine was one of the first to write history in a Christian interpretation. He saw that history was linear, not cyclical, and that God's plan was in all events. Augustine continually exalted the spiritual over the temporal in these matters. The two works of his considered to be greatest would be Confessions and City of God. In Confessions, books 1 thru 7 deal with his time before his conversion, 8 deals with the events surrounding his conversion. And 9 to 13 include some of his time after conversion and commentaries on the first chapters of Genesis. Confessions was written out of some crisis he or the church may have faced during that time (401). Many Christians have found blessing in Confessions, noting his sincerity and openness about his spiritual immaturity: 'Give me chastity and continency, but not too soon.' One of his other great works, City of God, was written sometime after the sack of Rome by Alaric in 410. It was in defense against the notion that Rome had been sacked due to the unfaithfulness to the old pagan ways. Abandoning the classics and following the Christian God brought judgment on Rome, they argued. Augustine wrote that the prosperity of the state did not depend on the faithfulness to the old gods, and that Rome suffered many tragedies before the coming of Christianity. He also argued that the Christian faith gave the people the temporal blessings they had enjoyed.

## 3. Thematic Analysis Matrices
### Theme: Methods of Biblical Interpretation
The text contrasts the Eastern grammatical/historical school, which emphasized literal meaning and grammar, with the Western allegorical school, noting how figures like John Chrysostom and Theodore of Mopsuestia rejected strained allegorical interpretations.

### Theme: Ecclesiastical Authority and State Relations
Exploration of how Church Fathers like Ambrose and Eusebius balanced their roles as church administrators or theologians with their interactions with secular power, such as Constantine the Great or Emperor Theodosius.

### Theme: Theological Influence and Legacy
An analysis of how figures like Augustine of Hippo synthesized classical philosophy with Christian theology, creating foundational works that influenced both the Reformation and Catholic doctrine.
