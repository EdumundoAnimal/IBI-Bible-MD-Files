The structural autonomy of the psalm is preserved by its inclusion as the literary pivot and shortest hymn within the Egyptian Hallel collection (Psalms 113–118).

### 2. Historical & Cultural Report

Occasion & Provocation Psalm 117 functioned litugically in the Second Temple period within the Egyptian Hallel (Psalms 113–118). These psalms were chanted during the major pilgrimage festivals (Shalosh Regalim): Pesach (Passover), Shavuot (Weeks), and Sukkot (Tabernacles), as well as during Hanukkah and Rosh Chodesh. At Passover, Psalms 113–114 were sung before the meal (prior to the second cup), and Psalms 115–118 were sung after the meal (over the fourth cup of wine). The historical occasion emerges from Israel's post-exilic reconstitution. Surrounded by imperial powers (Persian, Ptolemaic, Seleucid), the post-exilic community used this psalm to articulate a subversive, eschatological counter-narrative: the very Gentile empires that oppressed them would one day stand before the God of Israel in willing doxological surrender.

## 3. Thematic Analysis Matrices
### Theme: Covenant Universalism
The document argues that God's covenantal mercy, while historically displayed to Israel (ʿālênû), is inherently designed for global expansion, moving from the particular to the universal.

### Theme: Redemptive Doxology
Praise is presented not as blind emotionalism but as an objective response to God's historical acts of ḥesed (covenant love) and ʾemet (truth).

### Theme: Eschatological Ingathering
The psalm is interpreted as a prophetic charter for the ultimate inclusion of all nations, tribes, and languages into the worship of the Lamb, fulfilling the Abrahamic mandate.
