## 1. Verbatim Source Text
### Publishers

We are told from a young age that you are not supposed to judge a book by its cover, but knowing who has published a particular source may indeed tell you if it is worth your time. Here is a list of publishers you will encounter, running roughly from more scholarly to less academic: University Press (Cambridge, Oxford, Sheffield, Harvard, Yale, Chicago, etc.), Doubleday, Abingdon, Paulist Press, Augsburg, Scholars Press, Paternoster, T&T Clark/Continuum, Fortress Press, SPCK, Westminster/John Knox, InterVarsity Press (IVP), SCM, Hendrickson, Zondervan, Baker, Broadman & Holman, Moody. This list is not exhaustive: it does not cover every publisher. Thus you will need to do a little research and background reading to find out if a particular publisher is worth reading. This list is not etched in stone: there are a few newer Zondervan sources that are of very high quality and worth reading, and some older IVP sources that are frankly not academic at all. Moreover, this list is one man's interpretation: other teachers may disagree and you may come to find you do also. Read widely and use your own judgment. Your research should reflect the use of good, scholarly, critical sources (and critical is not a dirty word!). Therefore if at all possible your bibliography should contain more from the upper two-thirds of the list than from the bottom. Newer is not always better, but having more recent sources shows that you are incorporating recent scholarship and ideas in your work. Make sure a good percentage of your sources are relatively recent.

### Commentaries

It is important when doing academic research to distinguish between scholarly commentaries and devotional commentaries. Words in the title such as 'exposition', 'devotional', 'preacher' and 'sermon' will indicate that a commentary is not as suitable for academic purposes. This does not mean that these are worthless books! It means they cannot be the basis for your research. Consult D.A. Carson, New Testament Commentary Survey, and Tremper Longman III, Old Testament Commentary Survey for more detailed interaction on these issues. ### Types of Sources: Primary, Secondary, and Reference

There are several different types of resources available to you when doing research. Though these are not rigid distinctions, here is a broad overview of sources: Primary: Sources that contain original, non-interpreted or non-evaluated information. This can be almost anything, depending on your field of research. Secondary: Sources that analyse, evaluate and interpret the information contained within primary sources. These tend to be argumentative. Usually books, articles, and other writings by scholars and researchers. Reference (Tertiary): Sources that compile, analyse, and digest secondary sources. These tend to be factual, and include encyclopaedias, indexes, textbooks, dictionaries, introductions, etc. Work cycle: When researching, your approach will appear backwards: 1. Start with reference material to find out background information, summaries, and bibliographies of primary and secondary sources. 2. Find books, articles and other secondary sources providing more thorough analyses of your topic. Check to see what other scholars have been saying. 3. Having gained a solid background on your topic, you are better able to understand, interpret, and analyse the primary source information. Your research should show the use of all three types of sources in your bibliographies and footnotes.

### Origins of the Documentary Hypothesis (Hermeneutics/Pentateuch/Church History)

Reference: Handbook of Biblical Criticism (definition), An OT introduction or survey book, Church History or Hermeneutics Textbook. Secondary: Books describing, detailing and arguing for or against the documentary hypothesis. R.W.L. Moberly, The Old Testament of the Old Testament, Joseph Blenkinsopp, The Pentateuch. Primary: Works of Julius Wellhausen and others in the 18th and 19th centuries, J. Wellhausen, Prolegomena to the History of Israel. NB: It is not adequate to cite only reference and secondary sources that talk ABOUT Wellhausen and others who formed the Documentary Hypothesis: you need to cite the primary source itself. (Hermeneutics/Church History) John Calvin's interpretation of Gen. 1: Reference: Historical Handbook of Biblical Interpreters, Dictionary of Religion and Ethics, Church History Textbook (Caims, Gonzalez, etc.) - 1 vol., Church History Set (Schaff, Latourette, etc.) - multi-volume. Secondary: Books and articles on John Calvin, his life, his hermeneutics, etc., Alister McGrath, John Calvin, De Greet, The Writings of John Calvin. Primary: Calvin's commentaries, Calvin's sermons on Genesis. This is an interesting case: in one sense Calvin's commentaries are secondary sources because they are analysing the Bible; but they are also primary sources because they have been analysed and interpreted by many others through the centuries, and are the focus of the essay topic. Again, it is not good enough to write what others say about Calvin's interpretation, or even quote Calvin as quoted in a secondary source (a very tempting prospect!). You need to read and use Calvin's commentary yourself.

## 3. Thematic Analysis Matrices
### Theme: Source Hierarchy
The document establishes a clear hierarchy for academic research, distinguishing between primary, secondary, and reference (tertiary) sources. It emphasizes that researchers should start with reference materials to gain background, move to secondary sources for scholarly analysis, and ultimately engage directly with primary sources.

### Theme: Academic Rigor in Commentary Selection
The text warns against using devotional or 'preacher' commentaries for academic research, advising students to look for scholarly series like the ICC or NICNT. It explicitly states that titles containing words like 'devotional' or 'sermon' are not suitable as the basis for academic work.

### Theme: Critical Engagement
The document encourages students to read widely and use their own judgment rather than relying solely on a publisher's reputation. It highlights that 'critical' is a positive term in academic research and that students should avoid relying on secondary sources that merely summarize primary authors (e.g., citing Wellhausen through a secondary source instead of reading his primary work).
