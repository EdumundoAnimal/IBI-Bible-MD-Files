# Social and Religious World of Judea in the New Testament Era
**Author:** [[Unknown]]

## 1. Verbatim Source Text
### Introduction

The purpose of this essay is to describe the social and religious world of Judea and their effects on the New Testament world. This will be achieved by examining the social pressures Judeans experienced within the Greco-Roman world, and the religious pressure to conform. This essay will show how these factors influenced the rapid growth of the early Christian church in Judea which offered acceptance and truth with differing perspectives on Roman rule and Judaic religious life.

### Social pressures to belong

The Social pressures in the Greco-Roman society in Judea in the New Testament era can be broken down into four sections; (1) social networks, (2) honour and shame within the community, (3) moral depravation and (4) pagan/man worship in the form of Emperor/State worship which brought about the superstitious atmosphere of paganism. (1) Being part of social network such as an ethnic group, work group or religious cults was importance for feeling accepted. Unlike philosophy which depended on adhering to an ideology, attraction to Christian groups in the Greco-Roman world of Judea promised acceptance into an identity group probably through social ties. (2)Everett Ferguson in his book Background of early Christianity declares 'Classic culture was a shame culture as distinct from a guilt culture.' This was true in the group setting where an individual would bring either honour or shame to the group. Honour came from birth into a family of highly esteemed ancestry background, adoption into such family or by wealth. Honour could be earned by acts of benevolence or courageous acts in war (3) Christian and Jewish groups at the time would have considered the Greco-Roman world morally corrupt and that this sprang from idolatry. Philosophical teachings and rhetoric gave Roman citizens a code of conduct when relating to their gods, parents, the laws, foreigners, slaves, women and children. 'The Christian church was born into a world filled with competing religions which may have differed widely among themselves but all of which possessed one common characteristic - the struggle to reach a god or gods who remained essentially inaccessible.' ### Religious pressures to conform

In response to King Herod's oppressive rule, (over Judea in New Testament times), many Jews resorted to violent and non-violent means of revolt and protest for a number of reasons, (1) direct rule from Rome and (2) unbearable taxation. Frederick Murphy points out that; Emperor or state worship came from Hellenistic culture but was really only enforced by a few such as Caius Caligula who sought worship for himself in A.D. 40. This helped the ruling elite solidify control over the Roman world in a false bond of unity. Christians and Jews however would not worship Man (1Cor. 8:6) Merrill Tenney admits that 'Emperor Worship was a political rather than a religious cult.' Pressure towards Christians who did not conform was unrelenting and often resulted in crucifixion; this was due to Christian's common use of the words 'Christ is Lord' when greeting. The atmosphere of paganism was superstition, mythology and astrology alongside magic curses and blessings on enemies and friends.

### The Growth of Christianity

The economic situation deteriorated under Herod's rule with the rebuilding of the Temple in Jerusalem by the Jews. Overpopulation, famine and social upheaval increased alongside increased crime and unfair distribution of food and goods. The rejected sick, the marginalised poor and the shunned outcasts of society searched for acceptance groups. Men aggrieved by their situation turned to the Jesus Movement instead of Sacarii (dagger) terrorist resistance groups. Here they were taught to repent of their aggressive feelings to the opposing emotion of love instead of hate. Derek Tidball quotes (Matt. 9:36) showing compassion on the poor and helpless, becoming the true shepherd of the New Testament. Although free from religious obligation Christian Jews stayed amongst the Jewish community and continued in sacrifices and Temple prayer. Christianity offered freedom within Greco-Roman society as well as within the Jewish Religion. A new kind of freedom, Christian liberty, not freedom through revolt or the Greek understanding of self sufficiency, but gave it a new sense of 'Knowing oneself to be a creature of the creator.'

### Conclusion

This essay endeavoured to describe the social and religious world of Judea in the New Testament era. We can conclude that Christianity in the New Testament era started with small rural groups made up of rejected outcasts on the fringes of society. The social, political and religious hardships of the day contributed to the rapid growth of 'Jesus Followers', who sought acceptance and truth. We can see that these new Christians obeyed the Roman system and Jewish laws, bud did not submit to their false cults and religious heresies at the same time. The social and religious forces brought Judeans into bondage in New Testament times, where as Christianity brought liberty, James Dunne defines this kind of freedom as; '...freedom from the assumption that our social identity or our standing and reputation before others is more important than our standing before God, freedom from anxiety to have to prove ourselves and from fear of other peoples' opinions - in a word, freedom in God and from the world.' As the scripture says; 'So if the Son sets you free, you will be free indeed.' (John 8:36).

## 3. Thematic Analysis Matrices
### Theme: Honour and Shame Culture
The text identifies the Greco-Roman world as a 'shame culture' rather than a 'guilt culture,' where social standing was determined by birth, wealth, or ancestry, and individuals could bring either honour or shame to their group.

### Theme: Christian Liberty
The author contrasts Christian freedom with political revolt or Greek self-sufficiency, defining it as freedom from the need to maintain social reputation before others, and instead finding identity in God.

### Theme: Social Marginalization
The document argues that the rapid growth of Christianity was driven by the marginalized, poor, and sick who were excluded from the rigid social and religious structures of the time.
