# Old Testament Wisdom: Wisdom Literature Theme: The Tongue
**Author:** [[Éamon Parkes]]

## 1. Verbatim Source Text
### Introduction

This essay shall endeavour to define and limit the theme of the tongue and the lips as used in proverbs by examining the (1) historical context, (2) the literary content in order to (3) theologically critique the use of these words in Old Testament book of proverbs and the consequences of adhering to or ignoring this wisdom.

### Historical Context: The Teachings of Amenemope and Proverbs

Who wrote Proverbs? Most scholars accepted that King Solomon alone wrote the majority of Proverbs, and the copies made by the men of Hezekiah king of Judah. However it was not until the 1920's that and English Egyptologist discovered similarities in Proverbs with an ancient papyrus bought in Egypt in 1888. This ancient papyrus contained the 'Teachings of Amenemope' a collection of wisdom writings, composed in Ancient Egypt most scholars believe in the mid-1st millennium BCE. Consequently it was a German Egyptologist by the name of Adolf Erman who demonstrated that the teachings of Amenemope were closely paralleled with the portion of Proverbs 'words of the wise' found in Proverbs 22:17 - 24:22. To compare one examples, in relation to 'lying lips;' (Prov. 10:18) "He who conceals his hatred has lying lips, and whoever spreads slander is a fool." And The Teachings of Amenemope 16:1; "Do not act as witness to a false statement, Or remove another man by your tongue." What is notable here in the Hebrew and Egyptian languages is the use of lip and tongue as a metaphor for speech. Let us further examine this in a literary context.

### Literary Content: Lip and Tongue in Proverbs

The 'Collins Concise Dictionary' defines the lip as; "either of the two fleshy folds surrounding the mouth." The same dictionary defines the tongue as; "a movable mass of muscular tissue attached to the floor of the mouth in most vertebrates." However M. W. Meyer defines in the international standard bible encyclopaedia these organs as follows; "In general, Hebraic thought regarded lips not only as physical organs but also as organs of speech." He explains that the heart or the mind "directs the utterances of the lips," that it is rare for the lips or the tongue to act independent of these. An example of the lips/mouth terms used in the Old Testament would be in Exodus 6:12, 30; But Moses said to the LORD, "If the Israelites will not listen to me, why would Pharaoh listen to me, since I speak with faltering lips?" The KJV uses the words "uncircumcised lips", the NEB uses the words, "a halting speaker." Moses was a man of slow speech. The tongue, as Dr. McKnight translates James 3:8, "but the tongue of men no one can subdue," is an untameable wild animal that requires the help of God to tame. The tongue is personified in Isaiah 45:23b, "...Before me every knee will bow; by me every tongue will swear." By every tongue Isaiah means every man. In like manor metonymy is used by the King James translators in Pro 31:26b; "... in her tongue is the law of kindness," speech is implied here. Even when describing different languages, the writer of Genesis uses the words in Gen. 10:5, 20, and 31, "Everyone after his tongue." In literary terms the ancient East around the time when Proverbs was written used lips and the tongue extensively for all types of speech with the colourful use of language. Benjamin Franklin once wrote in his book Poor Richard Almanack, "The heart of a fool is in his mouth, but the mouth of a wise man is in his heart." In this next section we shall examine three ways of using or not using the tongue and a fourth section on the limitations of words.

### Theological Criticism

(1) 'Keeping a Tight Lip' when not to speak and when to break our silence. It is a wise thing to think before speaking. Proverbs shows us this in a number of ways; Primarily in Proverbs 13:3a Goldberg explains that to use many words in speech is unwise, the man is warned to "guard his lips" because in the long run it will "guard his soul." For if the man or woman has evil in his heart and he declines to pause and consider his words, the evil contents of his heart will be poured out, Pro. 15:28b. Ernest Lucas counsels for restrained in the amount of words we use, because of our imperfections we are inevitably going to say something harmful or hurtful, therefore prudence requires us to hold back from speaking gratuitously Prov. 10:19. Second we should always think first before we use our tongue. Proverbs 29:20 and in proverbs normally the word 'hasty' is not positively used. While the person may be well meaning, unconsidered words can damage as much as waving about a sword carelessly (Prov. 12:18). Thirdly it is essential to listen before opening our mouths as it says in Prov. 18:13, "He who answers before listening-- that is his folly and his shame." Nevertheless there are times when not speaking, especially in regard to injustices we are aware of, can be considered a sin, for example; when a person is depressed because of the pressures of life making one anxious, a kindly word 'said in season' will make glad and may even heal Prov. 12:25, "An anxious heart weighs a man down, but a kind word cheers him up." In the Royal British Navy there is a rule which states; "No officer shall speak discouragingly to another officer in the discharge of his duties." Even the British Navy understand the power of words to encourage or destroy morale. In a more extreme case of a person we know to be innocent on trial for his life or even the lost sinner hell bent for eternal judgement, our words when held back in silence could be considered a sin. (2) Evil speech, when words cause death and destruction. The tongue has the power of life and death," Prov. 18:21a. History has taught us that words spoken and unchallenged can cause the death of millions, like the evil radio chatter during the Rwandan genocide, where the Hutu tribesmen called for the killing of their Tutsi 'cockroach' neighbours, their fellow countrymen. Or Hitler's rhetoric and books like Mein Kampf where millions of Jews lost their live. James was right when he spoke of the tongue being a deadly fire set alight by hell (James 3:5-8). Even in Church a 'perverse tongue' can cause division and even proverbs 10:31b points out that an evil tongue must be stopped before its causes evil strife and division, "...a perverse tongue will be cut out." Like deadly poison Satan's speech deceived Eve before the fall. And what about the coming propaganda from the lips of the false prophet, prophesised by the apostle John in the book of Revelations, words can be used to mislead, control or devastate large groups of people. (3) Proper use of the tongue, when words bring life. Choosing the right words to say at the right times with the right motives bring a sense of gratification, (Prov. 18:20). (1) Words can refresh life spring waters, Proverbs 10:11a, "The mouth of the righteous is a fountain of life." God's word revealed through Christian live spoken by their testimonies can be like drinking the fresh water of encouragement. Proverbs 12:18b attests of that the use of the tongue by the wise can bring healing. And the sound doctrine of the Word preached correctly is health to a congregation, (1 Tim 1:10). (2) Words can benefit the one who speaks, (Proverbs 13:2a), "a proper use of the mouth will in the long run serve him well, and people will respect the righteous man for his few but well-chosen wise words." (4) The limitations of words. Pastor Chuck Swindoll in his book killing Giants reminds us of five ways that words alone can be ineffective; (i) when words are spoken by a fool, (Prov. 26:7), (ii) when they are spoken to a fool, (Prov. 17:4), (iii) when words are not followed by the appropriate deeds, (Prov. 14:23), (iv) when words are spoken contrary to God's truth, (Prov. 24:12), and (v) when words are spoken at inappropriate times at the wrong place, (27:14). Conclusion. This essay endeavoured to define and limit the theme of the tongue and the lips as used in proverbs by examining the historical, literary, theological use of the words in the Old Testament book of proverbs and to examine consequences of adhering to or ignoring such wisdom. The findings of this essay shed light on the consequences of use of the tongue. Used unwisely it can damage and when words are left unchallenged they can be the root cause of genocide, as history has proven. Correct and wise use of the lips can heal and build character and moral and can even be a source of life for the believer. Ephesians 4:29 best summarises the good use of words, these are wise words from the apostle Paul; Do not let any unwholesome talk come out of your mouths, but only what is helpful for building others up according to their needs, that it may benefit those who listen. As the Psalmist David once wrote in Psalm 141:3, "Set a guard over my mouth, O LORD; keep watch over the door of my lips." It is not within us to keep a watch over our tongue, it takes the divine intervention of almighty God to accomplish that.

## 3. Thematic Analysis Matrices
### Theme: The Tongue as a Metaphor for Speech
The document explores how the tongue and lips are used as metonymy and metaphors for human speech in both Ancient Egyptian wisdom literature (The Teachings of Amenemope) and the Old Testament book of Proverbs.

### Theme: The Power of Words
The author argues that words possess the power of both life and death. This is supported by biblical references (Prov. 18:21a) and historical examples, such as the role of propaganda in the Rwandan genocide and the rhetoric of Hitler.

### Theme: Prudence and Restraint
A central theological theme is the necessity of restraint in speech. The author cites Proverbs to argue that thinking before speaking, listening before answering, and guarding one's lips are essential practices for the wise.
