**Author:** [[C.F.D. Moule]]

## 1. Verbatim Source Text
### 104 PARTICIPLES

Sometimes the neuter participle with the article is virtually a noun (especially an abstract noun): τὸ ἀγαθόν, your good, your welfare, τὰ διαφέροντα (perhaps different 'values' or the things which are matter of value), τὸ κατέχον, the controlling (or restraining) factor, τὸ ὑπερέχον, the superiority. Cf. the Stoic τὸ συνετός, conscience, for which St. Paul uses the abstract noun συνείδησις. Note. As a rule the artistic distinction may be maintained between the participle with the article and the participle without, in such contrasts as: ὁ ὄχλος ὁ ἀνακράζων, the crowd which had assembled... and ὁ ὄχλος ἀνακράζων, the crowd when it had assembled... Yet, a passage like John xv. 2 makes one hesitate to dogmatize: is there really a distinction in this respect between πᾶν κλῆμα ἐν ἐμοὶ μὴ φέρον καρπὸν and πᾶν τὸ καρπὸν φέρον? It is just conceivable that the first means any branch, if it does not bear, rather than which does not bear, while the second certainly means which bears, but it is a questionable distinction. A further nuance of meaning is discernible when a participle becomes virtually an adjective by being placed between the article and the noun. Thus τὸν κοπιῶντα γεωργὸν, II Tim. ii. 6, practically means the hardworking farmer; whereas τὸν γεωργὸν κοπιῶντα would have meant (the farmer as he works (or worked), and τὸν γεωργὸν τὸν κοπιῶντα would have meant the farmer who works (or worked). An anarthrous phrase, κοπιῶντα γεωργὸν, would be ambiguous; it could mean a farmer as he works, a farmer who works, a hardworking farmer (or equivalents in other tenses, according to context). In other words, when noun and participle are both anarthrous, a participle may be adjectival or properly participial, according to context; e.g. ἀποθνήσκοντας ἀνθρώπους might mean dying men, or it might (as and context in Heb. viii. 8 clearly demands) be virtually adjectival and mean mortal men, men who are to die. In the same verse, μερτιπυομένους ὅτι σὺ ὦ bold and rather unusual way of saying (ἄνθρωπος τις) περὶ οὗ μερτιπυομένους ὅτι σὺ ὦ, one of whom witness is. In John xiv. 10, however, the A.V. and R.V. translations are no doubt intended to represent different (and different interpretations of the same text): ὁ δὲ πατὴρ μένων, αὐτὸς ποιεῖ... the R.V., the Father abiding in me doth his works is ὁ δὲ πατὴρ ἐν ἐμοὶ μένων ποιεῖ... (and the Father abiding in me doth his works of the article, irrespective of the αὐτός), the difference is made by the omission.

## 3. Thematic Analysis Matrices
### Theme: Participle Usage
The text explores the nuanced usage of participles in New Testament Greek, specifically focusing on the distinction between anarthrous (without article) and arthrous (with article) participles, and how they function as nouns or adjectives depending on context.

### Theme: Subordination and Negatives
The author discusses the use of 'μή' versus 'οὐ' with participles, noting that while 'μή' is the standard negative for participles, 'οὐ' is occasionally used, sometimes appearing to coalesce with the participle as an alpha privative.
