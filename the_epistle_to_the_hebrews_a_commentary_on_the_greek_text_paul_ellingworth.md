# The Epistle to the Hebrews: A Commentary on the Greek Text
**Author:** [[Paul Ellingworth]]

## 1. Cleaned Document Text
This document provides a comprehensive academic commentary on the Greek text of the Epistle to the Hebrews. It covers introductory topics including the authorship of Hebrews, its literary structure, theological themes, and the background of its intended readers. It investigates the author's use of the Old Testament and potential connections to contemporary religious movements like Gnosticism and the Qumran community. The work proceeds with a verse-by-verse commentary exploring Christology, the High Priesthood of Christ, and the exhortations to the first readers. Manuscript analysis and textual history are also included.

## 3. Thematic Analysis Matrices
### Theme: Christology
The commentary highlights Christ's superiority over angels, the Mosaic law, and the levitical priesthood, emphasizing his role as the eternal High Priest and Son of God.

### Theme: Theological Context
Exploration of the author's use of the Old Testament and interaction with diverse theological environments, including Gnosticism, Qumran, and Philo's Hellenistic Judaism.

### Theme: Pastoral Exhortation
Analysis of the purpose of the epistle as a pastoral address to early Christians facing potential apostasy, persecutions, and the need for perseverance.

## 4. Evidence Matrix & Verbatim Assertions
> "The christology of Hebrews has often in the past been analyzed in generally Chalcedonian categories. It is true that Hebrews strikingly combines, on the one hand, a reverential affection for the name of Jesus and a general interest in 'the days of his flesh' (5:7), and on the other hand, a strong emphasis on his present status at the right hand of God."
*Context Source: Introduction, p. 67*

> "The epistle which is inscribed to the Hebrews is received not only by the Churches of the East, but also by all Church writers of the Greek language before our days, as of Paul the apostle, though many think that it is from Barnabas or Clement."
*Context Source: Introduction, p. 37*
