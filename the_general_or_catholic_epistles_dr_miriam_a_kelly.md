# The General or Catholic Epistles
**Author:** [[Dr. Miriam A Kelly]]

## 1. Verbatim Source Text
### The General or Catholic Epistles: James, 1 and 2 Peter, 1, 2 and 3 John and Jude

This group of letters has been called the General or Catholic Epistles since the time of the Early Church Fathers because, unlike the epistles of Paul, they were considered to have been sent to the church at large rather than to particular churches. John's second and third letters are exceptions to this rule as they are really addressed to individuals. Nevertheless, because of their authorship and their value to all, they are treated along with the entire group.

### The Epistle of James

Its Recognition: The Epistle of James was, like other works placed in the antilegomena (contested books), not frequently cited by the ancients in their writings. It is thought that this is the reason why it was not, initially, readily accepted. However, it is more likely that the question over the identity of the author as well as the emphasis upon works were more at the base of the uncertainty. The latter was because, at first reading, James' teaching appears to contradict the Pauline doctrine of justification by faith. By the time of Jerome and Augustine, James had been accepted and at the 3rd Council of Carthage in AD 397 its inclusion in the Canon was recognised. At the time of the Reformation Erasmus questioned its inclusion, as did Luther, who called it 'a letter of straw'. The latter perhaps failed to fully grasp the implications of its message and saw conflict between James and Romans (the question regarding faith and or works was the issue).

### Authorship

For brevity's sake this study accepts that James the brother of Jesus, who became a believer after the resurrection of Christ, is the author of this letter. He was well known as a prominent leader in the Early Church. His first name and the introduction 'James, a servant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ' (James 1:1) would have been sufficient to establish his identity with believers at tat time (Gal 2:9,12; Jude 1; Acts 12:17; 15:13; 21:18).

### The Early Date of James

Most evangelical scholars today favour an early date. If James was martyred c. AD 62-63, then it had to be written before that. Some scholars believe that this was the first of the epistles and say that the internal evidence points to a date in the 40s. The Didache, also called The Teaching of the Twelve, or The Teaching of the Twelve Apostles, and The Teaching of the Lord, is an extracanonical document. Scholars disagree as to the date of its composition (80-120AD) but many believe it is early because of the content of its teaching). The leaders are called teachers (didaskaloi [3:1]) and elders (5:14). James writes about the believers gathering in the synagogue (2:2; NIV 'meeting'). Many scholars believe that the letter must have been written before the Jerusalem Council in AD 48-49 and that it is likely the very first document of the entire NT.

### Occasion and Date

The commencement of the letter must be taken to indicate the main reason for its necessity. James states: 1Consider it pure joy, my brothers, whenever you face trials

### Contents

This letter is sometimes criticised for its lack of theology. Although James is primarily practical in its emphasis it does not lack the basic building blocks of theological truth. It may not rise to the heights of Pauline or Johannine theology bit it does not lack essential truth. Clear statements concerning God and Jesus are not few. Most is said concerning God: God and Jesus are placed on the same level (1:1), God is unchanging (1:17), God is the creator (1:18), God gives the believer spiritual birth through the word of truth (1:18), God gives good gifts (1:12), God gives wisdom to believers who need it when they are tested (1:5), God chooses the poor in spirit to inherit his kingdom (1:5), God hears the cries of the oppressed (5:4), God calls men to a life of purity and faultless conduct (1:20,27), God does not tempt anyone but he does allow testing (1:13). It has long been recognised that the complete, or mature man, is the central theme of the letter. (Henry Alford, The Greek NT, Vol. IV, rev. E.F. Harrison (Chicago, Moody, 1968), 106). There is a need for the believer to persevere in the face of testing (1:6). The maturing process is manifest in the way in which the person reacts, or responds when confronted by difficulties; social standing is not important; what counts is moral behaviour, the exercise of mercy and the control of one's own tongue. There is more about the tongue in this epistle than elsewhere in the Scriptures. Real truth is exhibited in a wisdom, which bears witness to a life committed to God, but it is worked out in right relationships (3:13-18). This is where the apparent conflict arises between faith and works. It needs to be underlined that this conflict is only apparent because it is not real. Faith and deeds go together. Vain profession is not worth anything (2:14). It is easy to tell others how to live but it is not as easy to keep to one's own ideals. So teachers who expect others to adhere to their teaching need to make sure that they are not hypocritical and practice what they preach (3:1).

### Faith and Works

Note the discussion about faith and works in (Jam 2:14-26). Martin Luther called James 'an epistle of straw' because it seemed to contradict the teaching of Rom and Gal on justification by faith. However if we look carefully at what James is saying there is no argument between James and Paul. But note Tenney's comment: 'James is not really an attack on faith, but a protest against the hypocrisy of pretending to have faith without demonstrating it in works...James does not dent the necessity of faith. He insists that faith must produce results. Like Paul, he took his illustration from the life of Abraham, the founder of the Jewish race and the first beneficiary of God's covenant relations with his people. Paul cited this instance of Abraham's response to God's promise as proof that salvation is by faith, not of works (Rom 6:1-2). James used Abraham's sacrifice of Isaac to prove that faith must be manifested through works in order to be real (Jam 2:21-24)." (MC Tenney), 267. To put it simply, it may be said that when Paul talks about salvation by faith, he is emphasising the means of entry into relationship with God (justification); James is saying that if you are indeed justified by faith, then this should be manifestly obvious to everyone. The truth of justification by faith, and of works as the evidence of faith is supplementary, not contradictory. James counteracts the idea that divine grace allows men to live as they please- like Paul he is against it (Rom 6:1-2). The Lord Jesus taught, "By their frith you will recognise them" (Mt 7:20).

### The First Epistle of Peter: Authorship and Background

Authorship: 'Peter, an apostle of Jesus Christ' (1:1). The first verse of the epistle supports Petrine authorship, as does its sense of apostolic authority, although this is often questioned today. The story of Peter's discipleship is fully told in the Gospels, beginning with his call (Mk 1:16-20; Lk 5:1-11). On the day of Pentecost it is Peter who represents the other apostles and preaches the gospel with power. His ministry in the first part of the Acts compares with that of Paul in the latter part of the book. The apostle opened the door of the gospel to Jews (Acts 2) and Gentiles (Acts 10). Background: Some have suggested that this letter was addressed to Jews because the introduction commences with the words: 'To God's elect, strangers in the world' (1:1), literally 'sojourners of the dispersion'. However, there are indications from the terminology, which do not fit Jews ('Once you were not a people', 2:10), it must be thought of primarily as a letter to Gentiles. These believers lived in various provinces of Asia Minor. Some have thought that 'sojourners' is used to indicate that there were Gentile believers who had fled the persecution of Nero, but this is unlikely as there was also considerable systematic persecution in Asia Minor. Likely it may mean nothing more than believers scattered among the pagans, since similar wording in 2:11 would substantiate that understanding. But no one really knows who exactly it was written to. The Occasion of writing: The reason for the letter is not to be found in any direct declaration by the author but in the overall content. There is a general background of persecution and suffering throughout this letter. No specific persecution is identifiable form the descriptions made but there is sufficient indication that believers were under considerable duress. (Some scholars have attempted unsuccessfully to identify the persecutions with the rule of specific emperors. Although this persecution could fit with what took place at the time of Nero, the letter may refer to all persecution against the church). This letter is an exhortation to stand fast and to live exemplary lives in the face of whatever happened; suffering is a keynote of the letter. Note the references to 'all kinds of trials' (1:6), 'unjust suffering' (2:19), 'suffer for what is right' (3:14), 'suffer for doing good' (3:17). (Over 18 times words regarding suffering, trials and testing, are used in the context of Christ and the believer in this epistle). They are to live just and pure lives separated from the evils of this world. This is true holiness (1:15-16; 2:9). Suffering is not to be considered as an unusual thing for a Christian (4:12-14).

### Content: Peter's lessons from the life of Christ

"The personal experience of Peter with Christ is reflected in this epistle to his friends who were imperilled by impending persecutions. He had known the feeling of helplessness when he realised that Jesus was dead, but his hope had become living when Jesus rose (1:3). His reference to love for Christ (1:8) may recall Jesus' challenge to him, 'Do you love me more than these?' (Jn 21:15ff), and his exhortation to 'tend the flock of God' (1Pet 5:2) maybe an echo of Jesus' injunction to him to do the same thing (Jn 21:15-17). The command 'gird yourselves with humility' (1 Pet 5:5), means 'to put on a slave's apron', which may recall Jesus' girding himself with a towel to wash the feet of the disciples (Jn 13:4). Five times Peter speaks of the sufferings of Christ (1 Pet 2:23; 3:18; 4:1,13; 5:1) as if perhaps he were the scene of Gethsemane and the crucifixion left an permanent impression on him." (M.C.Tenney), 352f.

### Peter's Christology

The apostle refers to: Christ's death and resurrection, Christ as the living stone and chief cornerstone (2:1-8), the example of Christ, Jesus as Lord (3:15), Jesus as the chief Shepard (5:4). He also speaks of the triune nature of God (1:2), especially the Fatherhood of God (1:3,13-17), thus perhaps giving a sense of purpose and strength in suffering.

### Peter's apostolic authority

A continuous chain of injunctions runs through the epistle. Note: Be self-controlled, set your hope, be holy, live your lives, love one another. Crave pure spiritual milk, respect everyone, love the brotherhood, fear God, do not fear, and do not be frightened. Set apart Christ as Lord, arm yourselves, be clear-minded, do not be surprised, do not be ashamed and praise God. Clothe yourselves with humility, humble yourselves, be alert and resist the devil.

### 2 Peter

Authorship: The authorship of 2 Peter is one of the most hotly debated in the NT. It was included in the antilegomena (contested books) because, unlike the first epistle, its origins were hotly disputed from an early date. It was not quoted in the writings of the Fathers before the Fourth Century. However, on the grounds of internal evidence including Peter's own name (1:1) and the inclusion of material, cf. Carson, Moo and Morris, An Intro to the NT, 433-437; Donald Guthrie, NT Introduction, Leicester, IVP, 3rd edn, 814-848). 2 Peter has less external support for apostolic authorship than 2 Peter. However, consider the internal evidence: The writer calls himself Simon Peter, an apostle (1:1; 1 Pet 1:1). With others he was an eyewitness of the transfiguration (1:16-18). He infers a first letter by 'this is now my second letter' (3:1; is this 1 Peter?). The epistle has apostolic tone, that is, one of authority (3:1ff). The main objection to Petrine authorship of the epistle concerns the decided difference in vocabulary and style between it and 1 Peter.

### Destination

The words, 'Dear friends, this is now my second letter to you' (3:1) indicate that this epistle was sent to the same group as that addressed in Peter's first letter (if the Petrine authorship is accepted); in other words, it is sent to the persons designated as living in northern Asia Minor.

### Occasion

The contents make it clear that there were teachers propagating a form of dangerous heresy, which threatens to destroy their faith (2:1). The multiple references to 'Knowledge' indicate that this false doctrine is linked to a form of Gnosticism which, left unchecked, would express itself in antinomianism and immorality (ch.2). Peter senses that the end is drawing close for him (1:14) and wishes to warn these believers that they need to take swift and appropriate action to avoid the drastic results of accepting such teachers and their doctrines. The first epistle emphasised the need for perseverance in the face of persecution from without but the second is more a warning regarding the danger from within. The threat - in the form of Gnosticism - appears to become a reality by the time of Jude.

### Date and place of writing

The epistle may have been written about AD 65-67, as it is thought that Peter died about AD 68.

### Contents

Knowledge is a key factor in this epistle, with one form or another of the word used repeatedly. This makes it apparent that knowledge or an early expression of Gnostic teaching was part of the difficulty, which Peter wishes to address. True knowledge in this letter is only possible through a relationship with God and Christ (1:2,3,8; 2:20; 3:18). It must always result in holy lives, which are evidence of the grace of God (3:11,18). Such lives would have no truck with antinomianism (3:17).

### Evaluation

2 Peter 1:20,21 recognises the OT as God's Word: "Above all, you must understand that no prophecy of Scripture came about by the prophet's own interpretation. For prophecy never had its origins in the will of man, but men spoke from God as they were carried along by the Holy Spirit." (M.C.Tenney), 370. In similar terms to those of the first epistle, believers are to make their calling and election sure (1:10). The eschatological theme of judgment and the day of the Lord is important (3:7-13). Many disregard the possibility of the coming of the Lord (3:3ff). This letter is a warning to keep one's eyes open for false teachers and their doctrines. There are various heresies implied: As already stated a false understanding of knowledge was being introduced. This view led to a laxity in conduct and practical Christian virtues (1:5-9). The teachings introduced had minimised the person and role of the Lord Jesus Christ (2:1). The false teachers were concerned primarily with their own welfare (2:3a). They tended to be arrogant and may not have fully comprehended the implications of what they were doing (2:10-12,18a). They propagated teaching on freedom while they lived in bondage to their habits and sin (2:19). Their character was manifest through their immoral behaviour (2:14-15). These false teachers denied the coming of Christ (3:4). The implications of such teaching are vast. They also therefore, reduce Christianity to the level of a natural and questionable religion. They also therefore, reduce the entire Christian hope of a resurrection with all its accompanying hope. The entire Gospel message becomes nothing more than hypothetical.

### Jude: Authorship and Background

The similarities between Jude and 2 Peter: The relationship between 2 Peter and Jude is complex. The difficulty arises because the majority of the contents of Jude are found in 2 Peter although the words are not in any way copied verbatim (19 of Jude's 25 verses are found in 2 Peter) and the way in which they are used is different. Many scholars, nevertheless, believe that Peter used Jude as a source. However, since Jude says he recalls the words of the apostles and Christ (Jude 17) it may be the other way round. The matter is more complex than merely deciding which was the first to be written. It is necessary to look at the purpose for each letter. 2 Peter is concerned with the question of possible apostasy and the emergence of false teachers in their midst (2:1) while Jude concentrates on the denial of Christ (Jude 4). There are undoubtedly parallels between the two but the difference is a matter of emphasis. Finally, it is not possible to be dogmatic and state which author was dependent upon the other. It has also been suggested that there may have been a source common to both which is no longer in existence. (See Carson and Moo, An Intro to the NT), 437-438. In the end the answer is unclear.

### The author and date and place of writing

'Jude, a servant of Jesus Christ and brother of James' (v1). The author is generally taken to be one of the younger brothers of Jesus - 'James' being the elder of the Jerusalem church (Acts 15:13). The epistle gives no clear indication as to where or when it was written. But if Jude ministered to the Jewish churches of Palestine, it may well have been sent to them in the period just prior to the fall of Jerusalem? (M.C.Tenney), 371.

### Content

Jude writes to urge his readers to 'contend for the faith' (v3).

### The false teachers

The reference to 'godless men, who change the grace of our God into a licence for immorality and deny Jesus Christ our only Sovereign and Lord' (v4) indicates a group of antinomians, who were abusing the liberty of the gospel (Rom 3:8; 6:1). They denied the Lord Jesus (v4), and were insubordinate (vv8-10). They were immoral (vv7-8), ungodly and boasters (vv15-16). Note the use of the word 'ungodly'. The doom of the false teachers was certain (vv5-7,14-15), but they had to be confronted (v3).

### Evaluation

The value of the epistle is seen in vv. 20-25. Notice the so-called 'noble imperatives', which focus on the Spirit of God and the love of God: 'build yourselves up', 'pray in the Holy Spirit', 'keep yourselves in God's love'. And the doxology of vv. 24-25 is majestic! The importance of making a doctrinal stand: At this moment in time many sections of the Christian church are questioning orthodox Christian doctrines, and we may need to take the word to heart: 'Contend for the faith that was once for all entrusted to the saints'. Each church age ought to faithfully represent the Gospel to its generation.

### The Johannine Epistles: Background

The three Johannine epistles are treated collectively in this section. Not all scholars agree that they come from the same pen nor that they were written by the same author as that of the Fourth Gospel. There are reasons for this put forward by men like (C.H.Dodd, in his book The Johannine Epistles, Moffatt NT Commentaries, (London, Hodder and Stoughton, 1946), but most of the objections have equally plausible responses as to why they should be accepted as coming from the same author. (See Carson, Moo and Morris, An Intro to the NT), 448-450. One of the main objections is the introduction of the title, 'The elder', in 2 John 1 and 3 John. Some scholars maintain that this indicates that the author was not the same individual as the 'Apostle'. However, if an understanding of the respect of age in the eastern culture is grasped, there is no reason why 'elder' should not be used of John the Apostle. (There are still many places in the world where it is an honour to be called 'old' or 'elder' as it carries with it the connotation of wisdom, experience and respect. It is only in Western culture that this is not generally the case).

### Addressees

The first letter includes no indication of the specific group addresses. The second is to 'the chosen lady and her children' (v1). No clear identification is possible but it is most likely that there is a specific congregation in view. The third letter names a certain 'Gaius', who is known and loved by John, however, since Gaius was a very common name in the Empire at the time this does not benefit us greatly. Because John is identified with Asia Minor and the area around Ephesus it is generally thought that these epistles must have been sent to those in this general region.

### Occasion

The purpose of these epistles is primarily polemical. The contents make it clear that false teachers and their insidious doctrines were threatening the believers' faith. There are also indications from the instruction given that John was concerned about the spiritual state of the believers. There is therefore, in addition to the polemic attitude, an important pastoral undertone that needs to be carefully examined.

### Contents

Several references state openly why John is writing (1 John 1:4; 2:1,26; 2 John 12; 3 John 13-14), but it is not from these references alone that the purpose for the letters is to be understood. The First Epistle concludes by stating: 'I write these things to you who believe in the name of the Son of God so that you may know that you have eternal life' (1 John 5:13). However, perhaps John is primarily concerned with alerting his readers to the danger of those who are bent on leading them astray (2:26). He wants to keep them from sin (2:1) but at the same time wants his joy to be complete (1 John 1:4) in their adherence to the ways of God. It is apparent that this heresy here is a 'mishmash' of various teachings. A date is given of about A.D. 85. The Second Epistle is very brief. It also emphasises the importance of correct teaching and the fact that the author will soon visit these believers at which time he will expound upon doctrinal and other matters (vv 10-12). Date about A.D. 85-90. The Third Epistle has a similar tone. It touches on the good testimony of Gaius (vv. 5-6) and Demetrius (v12) but is also taken up with Diotrephes who has been acting in a high handed and dictatorial manner. His fault, apart from an over inflated opinion of his own person, was a failure to recognise other ministries, including John's (vv9-10). There is no direct mention of any heresy in this last letter but there is a danger that Diotrephes' attitude and practices may lead to error. Date about 90 A.D.

### Contents (continued)

Christology is central to the epistles of John. Christ is man's mediator and works in close unity with the Father (1 John 2:1). There is a failure, on the part of many, to recognise the true humanity of Christ, as well as difficulty over the relationship between the divine and human. The First Epistle addresses the teaching that God could not possibly become a real man with real human attributes. Early Gnosticism with its reductionist view of Christ and its dualistic understanding of matter as intrinsically evil and spirit as good, seems to be behind this false doctrine. Gnosticism was influenced by Greek dualism, which separated the mind (which was spirit or pure) from the body (which by nature was material or evil). John makes it very clear that he, along with the other disciples, lived with Jesus long enough to know his true nature. They ate with him and literally rubbed shoulders with him. He was not only human, but he was also the Son of God in the flesh (1 John 1:1-3). At the heart of the question is the matter of humanity's redemption (1 John 2:2; 4:10). If Christ was not a real man, then God did not become man, Christ did not suffer and the cross was a farce. Consequently, man has no one to pay the debt of his sin and remains eternally condemned. It is perhaps for this reason that John argues so vociferously. Although the emphasis is upon Christology, the Father-Son relationship is of major importance as is the relationship between the believer, Christ, and the Father. Similarly, the role of the Spirit is essential in this fellowship. Ethics also play an important role in these letters. Because of their relationship with Christ, God and the Spirit, the believing community gives evidence of a lifestyle and ethic which is opposite to that of the non-Christian community. Conduct is motivated by the love of God so that love is not to be expressed in mere words but in sincere action (1 John 3:18; 4:7-11). Knowledge is important in these epistles. It is likely that this emphasis is to counter the false emphasis upon a superior kind of knowledge, as it was understood by the proto-Gnostics. John is adamant that belief in the Son of God is all that is necessary and not some mystical form of knowledge. Knowing God, Christ, the truth, the truth, Christian responsibility and the person's ultimate destiny are all key themes in the epistles. Perhaps the purpose is to consolidate the believer's purpose and grant him full assurance of his relationship both to God and his neighbour. In Johannine thinking the way in which a believer interacts with his neighbour and especially with those of the household of faith is inextricably bound up with his relationship to God. Possibly this is as close as one can get to any direct teaching on the Church in John's writings. Proto-Gnostic Teachings: Scholars are quick to identify the doctrinal difficulties in the Johannine epistles with trends, which are chiefly described by the Church historians Irenaeus and Eusebius. They believe that John's purpose was to counter the tenets of the Corinthians and Cerinthus. Cerinthus taught that the man Jesus was human while the divine Christ was Docetism. The divine descended upon the human Jesus at the baptism and left him just prior to the crucifixion. Docetism held that Jesus only seemed to be a man but was, in fact, pure spirit. (Docetism comes from the Greek verb dokeo, which means 'it appears' or 'it seems' to be). Cerinthus was more concerned with the combination of the human and divine in Jesus Christ. The problem is that it is not absolutely certain that this is the exact form of error John has in mind in these epistles. What can be suggested is that it is likely that there are some similarities between these heresies and some of what concerns John.

### Basic themes of Johns epistles

The theme of 1 John seems to be fellowship; fellowship with the Father is built upon the truth and, when experienced, keeps a person from doctrinal and moral error. The theme of 2 John seems to be about abiding in the truth. Abiding in the truth is essential for correct living, which includes avoiding error. Twelve times in this brief letter John uses the words 'truth', 'commandment', and 'teaching'. The theme of 3 John appears to be that the letter emphasises the demonstration of truth or error in the believer's life.

## 3. Thematic Analysis Matrices
### Theme: Justification by Faith and Works
The text argues that there is no contradiction between Paul and James. Paul focuses on the means of entry into relationship with God (justification), while James emphasizes that true faith must be manifested through works to be real. James is a protest against hypocrisy, not an attack on faith itself.

### Theme: The Nature of Suffering
In 1 Peter, suffering is presented as a keynote of the Christian life. It is not an unusual thing for a Christian, and the letter serves as an exhortation to stand fast and live exemplary lives in the face of duress.

### Theme: Proto-Gnosticism and Knowledge
The Johannine epistles and 2 Peter address false teachings that emphasize a 'superior' or 'mystical' knowledge. These teachings often led to antinomianism (immorality) and a denial of the true humanity of Christ (Docetism).

### Theme: The Importance of Fellowship
1 John emphasizes fellowship with the Father as a safeguard against doctrinal and moral error. This fellowship is inextricably bound to how a believer interacts with their neighbor.
