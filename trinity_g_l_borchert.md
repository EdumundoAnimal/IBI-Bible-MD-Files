But most importantly, in the sometimes desperately difficult middle books of De trinitate Augustine invokes so strong a version of the Neo-Platonic doctrine of simplicity as to blur, or even efface, the trinitarian distinctions he elsewhere maintains. In God there cannot be "three species of the one essence" (vii.6.11). The only way to distinguish the persons is in terms of the self-relations of the divine essence (viii.1), for each person is identical (not just identified) with that essence (v.10.11). At this point he develops the final approach of Gregory's Non tres dei, i.e., undifferentiated nature and different causality. For Gregory the trinitarian mystery was how we can have three persons and still say "one God." Augustine's problem was the opposite. He wanted to know how the identity of the Father, Son, and Spirit with the divine essence allows us still to talk of three persons (De trin. vii.4.8). Where Gregory released tension by saying "one man" in the human analogue (willing to be implausible on oneness to protect threeness), Augustine did so by giving less significance to threeness in the divine analogue (willing to be vague on threeness to protect oneness). In God, says Augustine, there must be "three somethings" (De trin. vii.4.9). Augustine's trinitarian mystery is really quid trev (three what?) (v.9.10), and his explanation of it piously indeterminate for both he and Gregory believed that the trinitarian mystery — however stated — surpasses comprehension and resists human resolution (xv.51). C. Medieval Theories. Augustine's massive influence was to shape all succeeding Western trinitarianism. In the 6th cent. Boethius defined persona as "an individual substance of a rational nature," but he did so in a christological treatise (Liber contra Eutychen et Nestorium iii). His De sancta trinitate simply compresses Augustine: trinitarian oneness is determined by considerations of simplicity: trinitarian distinctions, whether substantial nor accidental, are relative. Father, Son, and Spirit are in fact names for the self-relations of the divine "thing" (id quod est) or essence. Thus, just as a given weapon can be called a sword, brand, or blade, the divine essence can be called Father, Son, and Spirit (De sancta trin. iii). In the seventh-century Creed of the (11th) Council of Toledo, one can see an early example of Augustine's influence on the filioque controversy. Augustine had suggested a love analogy for the trinity: Sun, and Spirit are like a lover, his beloved, and the love they share (De trin. viii.10.14; ix.2f.). Naturally, then, the Spirit as the bond of love (vinculum caritatis) between the Father and the Son "is shown to have proceeded at the same time from both, for he is to be acknowledged as the love or sanctity of both" (11th Council of Toledo; Denzinger, p. 527). The Council thus concluded that the Spirit is from the Father and the Son (filioque), although Augustine himself would have said "originally" from God the Father (De trin. xv.29.47f.). The Eastern Church rejected the Western practice (ca. 625-1000) of including the filioque clause in liturgical uses of the Nicene Creed for several reasons: it is absent from the key biblical text (Jn. 15:26), it depends on a given theologian's theory, and it seemed presumptuous for Benedict VIII (the Western pope who officially added the filioque claim in 1014) to alter a creed set by an ecumenical council. The resulting schism (1054) between East and West remains unhealed to this day despite discussion of a possible compromise with the phrase "through the Son."

### The difficulties in delfinition

The 12th and 13th cents. saw the apex of Western Augustinian trinitarianism. In his De trinitate, Richard of St. Victor at once developed the Augustinian analogy of love in a new direction and consummated a great twelfth-century fascination with mystical love, as in Bernard of Clairvaux, Achard of St. Victor, and Richard's teacher Hugh of St. Victor. Richard's theory in nuce is that all love needs both a giver and a receiver. Agapé or caritas is by nature other-directed. Yet a creature would be an insufficient object for God's love. God therefore cannot fully love unless there are two persons in God Himself. But a good being is willing to share two-personed love with a third. Thus, Richard's remarkable conclusion: "In order for charity to be true, it demands a plurality of persons; in order for charity to be perfected, it requires a trinity of persons" (De trin. iii.13). These are "three incommunicable existences" of the one divine essence. Richard stands somewhat outside the line that runs from Augustine through Boethius and the Athanasian Creed to Thomas Aquinas (1225-1274). The latter explicitly rejected Richard's theory that self-love is not properly love at all, maintaining that this is true only of creatures who have niches and needs to fill (Summa theol. i.32.1). Aquinas here followed Augustine, who had taken seriously the possibility of unipersonal divine self-love as a trinity pattern reflected in single human minds; for Augustine the human mind's capacity for remembering, understanding, and loving itself is an image of God (De trin. xiv.8.11). Aquinas's theory climaxed the Western tendency to stake down divine oneness, usually expressed in philosophical terms, and to view trinitarian distinctions as self-relational. The Fourth Lateran Council (1215) had said that in God there is "one highest, incomprehensible and ineffable thing" and "each of the three Persons is that thing" (Denzinger. p. 804). By this stage of Western thought, tritheism meant not just Arianism, but any denial of simplicity. Indeed, considerations of simplicity determined biblical exegesis: "When the Truth prays to the Father for his disciples, 'that they may be one, as we also are' (John 17:23) the word 'one' as applied to the disciples is to be taken in the sense of a union of charity in grace, but in the case of the divine persons in the sense of unity of identity of nature" (Denzinger, p. 806). In his own theory Aquinas spoke of three subsistences or persons in God, relationally distinguished according to the two movements of generation and procession, which Aquinas understood as divine acts of understanding and willing, or of Word and Love (Summa theol. i.27.5). Each person is his own relation (i.39.1), and each person is identical with the whole divine essence, for "everything which is not the divine essence is a creature" (i.28.2). Are the persons really identical with each other, then, since they are each identical with the divine essence? Aquinas denied this conclusion (28.4), claiming that there is still "relational" or "logical" distinction. Suppose, for instance, that Thomas thinks of himself. Though thinker and the one thought are really identical, they are relationally or logically distinct, since the thinker is an active subject and the one thought is a passive object. Thomas believed that such distinctions, although relational, were nonetheless real, and that similarly, Father, Son, and Spirit could be said to be really distinct if relationally distinct. Like Augustine, of course, he constantly stressed the incompleteness of every analogy (e.g., i.27.1). D. Modern Trinitarianism. ## 3. Thematic Analysis Matrices
### Theme: Augustinian Trinitarianism
The text highlights Augustine's reliance on Neo-Platonic simplicity, which creates a tension between maintaining trinitarian distinctions and the absolute oneness of the divine essence. Augustine's approach is described as 'piously indeterminate,' focusing on the 'quid trev' (three what?) mystery.

### Theme: Relational Distinction
The document traces the development of 'relational' or 'logical' distinction in the West, from Boethius's definition of 'persona' to Aquinas's view that persons are distinct through the movements of generation and procession (understanding and willing), while remaining identical to the divine essence.

### Theme: The Filioque Controversy
The text explains the Western addition of the 'filioque' clause to the Nicene Creed, rooted in Augustine's love analogy (Spirit as the bond of love between Father and Son), and the resulting schism with the Eastern Church, which viewed the addition as unbiblical and ecclesiastically presumptuous.

### Theme: Richard of St. Victor's Love Analogy
Richard of St. Victor argued that true charity requires a plurality of persons, positing that God must be a trinity of persons to perfect the nature of love, distinguishing his view from the unipersonal self-love models of Augustine and Aquinas.
