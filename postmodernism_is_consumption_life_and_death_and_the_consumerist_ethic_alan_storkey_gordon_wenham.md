# Postmodernism Is Consumption / Life and Death and the Consumerist Ethic
**Author:** [[Alan Storkey / Gordon Wenham]]

## 1. Cleaned Document Text
The provided text is a collection of two scholarly articles exploring the intersections of consumerism, postmodern culture, and Christian theology. The first part, 'Postmodernism Is Consumption' by Alan Storkey, argues that consumerism has become a secular religion, replacing traditional faith with the pursuit of individual gratification and self-worship. It analyzes how economic theories like utility and neoclassicism have shaped modern human behavior, leading to the fragmentation of relationships and the loss of objective moral truths. The second part, 'Life and Death and the Consumerist Ethic' by Gordon Wenham, shifts focus to the practical implications of this consumerist mindset on ethics, specifically regarding Sunday observance, marriage, reproductive technologies, abortion, and euthanasia. It contends that the consumerist drive for self-autonomy and immediate satisfaction contradicts the biblical teachings on life as a gift and the sacrifice inherent in true faith.

## 3. Thematic Analysis Matrices
### Theme: Consumerism as Secular Religion
The authors argue that modern consumerism functions as a dominant belief system or 'faith' that dictates daily life, rituals, and values, effectively competing with and displacing Christian worship.

### Theme: The Impact of Neoclassical Economic Theory
An analysis of how economic models focusing on utility maximization, individual autonomy, and the 'rational actor' have contributed to a cultural shift toward self-centeredness and the dissolution of communal ties.

### Theme: Biblical Ethics vs. Consumerist Autonomy
The contrast between the biblical understanding of life as sacred and communal and the consumerist view that prioritizes individual choice, 'self-creation,' and the elimination of discomfort or 'bad' outcomes.

### Theme: The commodification of life
The discussion of how reproductive technologies and modern medical ethics treat human life, birth, and death as products or services to be controlled and managed for individual convenience.
