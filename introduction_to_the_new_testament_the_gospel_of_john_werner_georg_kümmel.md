The most striking distinction between the speeches of the Johannine Jesus and those of Jesus in the Synoptics is the completely different language which Jesus speaks in Jn. It is not so much a matter of language in the grammatical-stylistic sense as of its conceptual world. Of course, the language of Jn in the narrower sense raises problems too: indeed, on the basis of the strongly Semitic character of the language of Jn, it has often been conjectured that Jn as a whole is a translation from the Aramaic. Occasionally it has been proposed that only one of the sources drawn upon by the author was written in the Aramaic language. Bonsirven, however, has drawn attention to the many features in the Greek of Jn which cannot be accounted for on the basis of translation from Aramaic, while Colwell has shown kinship of the language of Jn to Epictetus, and Kilpatrick has pointed out the similarity to the language of the LXX. Barrett has shown that it is not necessary to adduce mistranslations; but even in rejecting the idea of a translation of Jn in the Aramaic, it must be acknowledged either that the author thought in Aramaic while writing in Greek, or at least that he came from a bilingual area. The real historical problem, however, is the origin of the conceptual world in which, in Jn, the evangelist himself is speaking, as well as Jesus, the Baptist, and the Jesus. ### 5. The Aim and the Theological Character of the Gospel of John

The aim pursued by the author of Jn is expressed by him in the conclusion (20:30 f): "Many other signs did in the presence of his disciples which are not written in this book. These [signs], however, are written that you might believe, that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that in believing, you might have life in his name." It cannot be decided with certainty whether in 20:31 πιστεύσητε (68 vid, 87, 0, 0250) or πιστεύητε (the majority of MSS) is original, the present tense. But even if πιστεύητε is original, the purpose of these words is to include certainty as to whether Christian readers are presupposed who are to be strengthened in the faith, or men who are to be won.

### 6. The Author

Since the days of the Alogoi, but especially since the beginning of the nineteenth century, the question about the authorship of Jn has been loaded. On the one hand, the theory that it was written by John the son of Zebedee as named in the tradition has been passionately defended, because it has been thought that on this tradition hangs the apostolic authority and the historical reliability of Jn. On the other hand, this tradition has been just as passionately contested, because the historical or theological doubtfulness of this Gospel is seen as based on the untruth of this tradition. Since both lines of argument are false, the discussion of the question of authorship should seek dispassionately to determine what we can know and what we cannot. In Chs. 1-20 Jn does not name the author, and where the author includes himself with others in a "we" he does not indicate that he is an eyewitness, as we have already seen. In the sense that the author of the Gospel we do meet the disciple of ἦν ἀγαπᾶ ὁ Ἰησοῦς (13:23-25; 19:26 f; 20:2-8), but the Gospel itself offers no help in identifying this person. All that is clear is that this "beloved disciple" stands in some sort of relationship with Peter. Beyond that one may ask whether the ἄλλος μαθητής (18:15 f) who, as an acquaintance of the high priest, leads Peter into the high priest's palace, is identical with the "beloved disciple" because that person is later indicated as ὁ ἄλλος μαθητής. But since this designation in 20:3 refers back to τὸν ἄλλον μαθητὴν ὃν ἐφίλει ὁ Ἰησοῦς, the question as to the identity of ἄλλος μαθητής in 18:15 f cannot be answered unambiguously. If the beloved disciple is meant here, then what is implied is the superiority of more certain in 19:26. It does not help us further to learn in 18:15 f an acquaintance of the high priest. Potentially of greater significance would be another passage if its logical connection were more certain. In 19:26 the beloved disciple is mentioned as standing at the cross, together with the women, to him, Jesus entrusts his mother. Then are reported the death of Jesus and the piercing of his death by the soldiers, and the lance thrust (19:28-34). Then follows in 19:35 the statement "and he has seen and blood flow from Jesus' side" opened by the lance thrust (19:28-34). Then follows in 19:35 the statement "and he has seen and he has testified to it, and his witness is reliable, and he knows that he tells the truth, by which also you believe." Since the only one present at the cross in addition to the women is the beloved disciple, it is plausible to see him as the one mentioned in 19:35, and the witness in 19:35, even though that is not directly stated. But it is still remains unclear whether ἐκεῖνος in v. 35b and ἐμαρτύρησεν are correct, 179 although the equating of ἐκεῖνος and ἐμαρτύρησεν is correct—then it may be inferred that the evangelist can name an eyewitness (at least of the Jesus' death) as a guarantor. But even if all this is correct, to what extent we can presuppose that this beloved disciple was a witness of the scenes in which he is mentioned still remains completely unknown, and who he is remains unknown. According to 19:35, he can scarcely be identical with the author. In the perspective of Jn 1-20 we can go no further, so that Jn is anonymous.

## 3. Thematic Analysis Matrices
### Theme: The Johannine Conceptual World
The author argues that the language and conceptual world of the Gospel of John (e.g., light/darkness, truth/lie, above/below, the 'I am' sayings) differ significantly from the Palestinian-Jewish conceptual world of the Synoptic Gospels, suggesting a different historical-religious setting.

### Theme: The Aim of the Gospel
The author identifies the purpose of the Gospel of John as stated in John 20:30-31: to provide signs so that readers might believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and find life in his name, whether they are already believers needing strengthening or non-believers needing conversion.

### Theme: Authorship and the Beloved Disciple
The text concludes that the Gospel of John is anonymous. While the 'beloved disciple' is mentioned in the text, the author argues that the text does not provide sufficient evidence to identify this figure with John the son of Zebedee or any other specific historical person, and that the 'beloved disciple' is likely not the author himself.
