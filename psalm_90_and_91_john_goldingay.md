# Psalm 90 and 91
**Author:** [[John Goldingay]]

## 1. Verbatim Source Text
### Psalm 90: Translation

Plea. Moses's, God's man. 1My Lord, you were a shelter; you were ours, generation after generation. 2Before mountains were birthed and you brought forth earth and world, From age to age, you were there, God. 3You would turn mortals to crushing; you said, 'Turn, you people!' Because a thousand years in your eyes were like a day, yesterday when it passes, or a watch in the night. 5You swept them away in sleep, though in the morning they could be like grass that can renew itself. 6In the morning it can flourish and renew itself; by evening it can wither and dry up. 7Because we are spent through your anger, through your fury we have been overwhelmed. 8You put our wayward acts in front of you, our youthful deeds in the light of your face. 9Because all our days have passed under your wrath, we have spent our years as moaning. 10The days of our years in themselves are seventy years, or, with strength, eighty years. But their boisterousness has been toil and trouble, because it has passed quickly and we have flown, and in accordance with your wrath, for your wrath? 12In counting our days, so make us acknowledge it, in order that we may acquire a wise mind. 13Turn, Yhwh, how long? relent over your servants. 14Fill us in the morning with your commitment, so that we may resound and rejoice all our days. 15Make us rejoice in accordance with the days you have afflicted us, the years we have seen evil. 16May your action be seen by your servants, your majesty for their descendants. 17May the delights of my Lord come, our God, for us; Establish the work of our hands—establish it, yes, the work of our hands—establish it.

### Psalm 90: Interpretation

The Church of England Book of Common Prayer specifies the use of Ps. 90 or Ps. 39 at a funeral service; this takes up the psalm's meditation on the transitory nature of human life. Already in pre-Christian times, Jubilees 23.12-15 picks up its language in commenting on the shortening of the human life span in Genesis and the theme of mortality has been the focus of much Christian study of it. But modern liturgical revision shortens the psalm so as to omit some of the references to God's wrath and some or all of the plea in the last section, and this reflects the fact that such use of the psalm, while appropriate and helpful, does not correspond to the inherent meaning of the psalm as a whole. Its last prayer psalm, even Luther opens his commentary on it by describing it as 'the chief prayer of Moses', though he interprets it christologically. These last verses make explicit that the psalm is a prayer arising out of the people's misfortunes. This is also implicit in the lament or protest of the earlier part. Hardship has gone on year after year, perhaps decade after decade, century after century (though one should not be literalistic in interpreting the time references; trouble can seem to last an eternity when chronologically it lasts a relatively short time). The opening and closing 'my Lord' may suggest that the community prayer is uttered by a person such as a king or governor, or by a minister. It parallels Ps. 85, and overlaps with the prayers in Ezra 9, Neh. 1 and 9, and Dan. 9. It has a number of the features of a prayer psalm: after the opening invocation, it looks back on how things once were (vv. 1-5), contrasts that with how things are (vv. 6-12), then pleads with God to restore blessing (vv. 13-17).

## 3. Thematic Analysis Matrices
### Theme: Divine Eternity vs. Human Transience
The psalm contrasts the eternal nature of Yhwh, who existed before the mountains were birthed, with the fleeting nature of human life, which is compared to grass that withers by evening or a watch in the night.

### Theme: Divine Wrath and Human Sin
The author argues that human suffering, toil, and the brevity of life are linked to God's anger and fury in response to human waywardness and youthful deeds.

### Theme: Plea for Restoration
The final section of the psalm shifts from lament to a plea for God to relent, fill the people with commitment, and establish the work of their hands, reflecting a desire for divine favor to replace the years of affliction.
