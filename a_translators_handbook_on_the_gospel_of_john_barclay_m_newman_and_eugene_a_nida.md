# A Translator's Handbook on the Gospel of John
**Author:** [[Barclay M. Newman and Eugene A. Nida]]

## 1. Verbatim Source Text
### 14.30

I cannot talk with you much longer, because the ruler of this world is coming. He has no power over me, I cannot talk with you much longer, because the ruler of this world is coming. Most translations likewise render this statement literally in the future tense. The temporal modifier much longer must be understood in the sense of a few hours at most, since it was later that same night that Jesus was betrayed and arrested. The ruler of this world was first mentioned in 12.31 (see there). He has no power over me is literally "And in me has nothing." This rendering is also that of Gdsp, RSV, and JB. Most other translations (Phillips, NAB) NEB has no hold on me or something similar. (See Mft, Pips, NAB) NEB has no claim on me, has rights over me, which is not entirely clear. It is probably better to use the equivalent Hebrew, which means "to have no claim upon someone." The NEB supports the meaning given by TEV and most other translations. He has no power over me may be rendered "He is not able to control me" or "to command me."

### 15.1-4

15 "I am the true vine, and my Father is the gardener. 2 He breaks off every branch in me that does not bear fruit, and every branch that does bear fruit, he prunes, so that it may bear more fruit. 3 You have already been made clean by the teaching I have given you. 4 Remain united to me, and I will remain united to you. A branch cannot bear fruit by itself, it can do so only if it remains in the vine. In the same way you cannot bear fruit unless you remain in me.

### 15.1

These verses are generally considered to be a unit; the imagery of the vine in this chapter is last mentioned in verse 16, and there is a definite change of subject between verse 17 and 18, although the difficulty of verses 1-17 is recognized by New Testament scholars. There are differences of opinion as to where the divisions within this section take place. Some make the major break after verse 10, while others make it after verse 8, and still others after verse 6. Wherever the break is made, it is recognized that the first part of the section deals with the parable of the vine, and the second part deals with the application. For the purpose of analysis, we shall divide this section into two major parts (1-6, 7-17), although in doing so we depart somewhat from TEV's presentation of the text. TEV divides this section into three paragraphs (verses 1-4, 5-10, and 11-17). Verse 1 introduces himself as the real vine (an intimate link that Israel is the "vine" of God and his Father as the gardener. Verse 2 depicts the two activities of the gardener: breaking away the branches that cannot bear fruit, and pruning every branch that does bear fruit. Verse 3 interrupts the parable, assuring the disciples that they are already "clean" and so need not fear being "cut away." Verse 4 is an exhortation for them to remain in union with Christ, from whom they receive their life. Verse 5 reproduces the thought of verse 4, stating positively the cutting thought that verse 4 states negatively. Verse 6 concludes the parable of the vine, indicating the fate of the useless branches. Verses 5-6 present the alternatives of remaining or not remaining in Jesus, while verses 7-17 develop only the positive side of remaining. This section is explicit that Jesus is speaking to his disciples (verse 8). Verses 7 and 8 go closely together, in that verse 8 is the fulfillment of what is asked for in verse 7. The theme of love, developed in verses 9-17, explains the theme of fruit bearing (verse 8). In verse 9 Jesus affirms that the love he has for the disciples is the same as that his Father has for him. In verse 10 he affirms that the disciples will remain in his love, if they obey his commands, just as he has. In verse 11 he says that the love he has for them is the reason for his joy. In verse 12 he repeats the command to love one another, and 13 and 14-15-17 explain some results of mutual love. Verse 16 indicates love as the basis for the choice which results in fruit bearing. Verse 17 brings together the second division again: the command to love, thus tying together the section headings 12-17 of this section. The section heading, The Real Vine, poses two problems of translation and interpretation. It is an appositional expression, in which the Real Vine is in apposition to Jesus. Second, the Real Vine is a figurative metaphor and in some languages may require identification as a figurative expression by some marker. For example, one can make this metaphor into a simile by saying "Jesus is like the real vine" or "Jesus as the real vine." It is also possible to use the expression "the true vine" or "the true vine" as a metaphor. Jesus requires careful translation and interpretation; it is an appositional expression.

## 3. Thematic Analysis Matrices
### Theme: The Parable of the Vine
The text identifies the vine imagery as a central metaphor for the relationship between Jesus, the Father, and the disciples, emphasizing the necessity of remaining in union with Christ to bear fruit.

### Theme: Fruit Bearing and Pruning
The gardener (the Father) performs two actions: breaking off branches that do not bear fruit and pruning those that do, to ensure greater productivity. This is linked to the disciples' obedience to Jesus' commands.

### Theme: The Command to Love
Love is presented as the foundational theme for the disciples' relationship with Jesus and one another, serving as the basis for their choice and their fruit-bearing capacity.
