# Friendship
**Author:** [[G. J. Brooke]]

## 1. Verbatim Source Text
### Friendship

Friendship constituted a regular topic of discussion in ancient literature, although specific views on friendship varied in different periods, places and authors. Some of the ideals of friendship impact our understanding of NT passages even where the specific term is unused.

### 1. Kinds of Friendship in Antiquity

Friendship was a regular ancient topic of discourse (e.g., Epictetus Disc. 2.22), the leading subject of numerous essays, for instance, by *Aristotle (Eth. Eud. 7.1234b-1246a; Eth. Nic. 8-9); Chrysostom (Third Letters, Mor. 934-97B); Dio *Cicero (De Amic.); Seneca (Ep. Lucul. 3, "On Friendship"; see further Fitzgerald, 172-77); and Theophrastus (according to Aulus Gellius Noc. Att. 1.3.10-11). Scholars have produced detailed studies of friendship in *Philo, who develops some *Stoic ideals (see Sterling); on the Peripatetics (Schroeder, 45-56; for other sources, see especially Fitzgerald 1997b, 7-10). Even before Aristotle, many ideals of friendship circulated that later became pervasive in the Roman world (see Fitzgerald 1997a).

### 2. Ideals for Friendship in Antiquity

Although Roman paternal friendship made at best a vague pretense to equality, this traditional Greek image of friendship, even when related to benefaction, demanded at least the idea of an equality of reciprocal good-will (Diogenes Laertius Vit. 7.91, and is said to have "defined friendship as an equality of reciprocal good-will" (Diogenes Laertius Vit. 5.31, as translated in LCL 1:478-79). Of course, what Aristotle meant by "equality" differs considerably from our usage of that concept. Any kind of friendship could exist either between equals or with one as a superior (Aristotle Eth. Eud. 7.3.2, 1238b; 7.10.10, 1242b; Eth. Nic. 8.7.1, 1158b; 8.13.1, 1162ab); Aristotle further defined "equality" more proportionately than quantitatively (Aristotle Eth. Nic. 8.7.2, 1158b). In the same way, his teacher *Plato stressed both the friendship held by loving equals and that which stemmed from the poor's need for the rich (Plato Leg. 8, 837AB). Nevertheless, equality remained part of the traditional Greek ideal of friendship. As early as Homer a leader could honor a special friend above his other companions, regarding him as equal to himself (Homer Il. 18.81-82). Others could speak of a friend as "another I" (Diogenes Laertius Vit. 7.1.23); Neo-Pythagorean tradition stressed friendship as equality (see Thom). Alexander and Jewish writers also picked up on this; *Epistle of Aristeas 228, the highest honor is to be shown to parents but the next honor to one's soul (Hadas, 189). This view continued to affect popular thought. Thus one letter recommends a friend (amicum) by exhorting the receiver to view him "as if he were me" (P. Oxy. 32.5-6, 2d cent. A.D.); this ideal may inform the background of Philemon 17-19.

### 3. Dying for Friends

Loyalty to friends and treating friends as one's own equals, as another self, might require dying for them. Every Jewish source prohibits sacrificing another to spare one's own life but allowed that one's life takes precedence over another's life (Akiba in Jacobs, 42-44). Nevertheless, though one was not required to love one's neighbor more than oneself, Judaism did praise as heroic those persons who would sacrifice their lives on behalf of their friends (Jacobs, 47).

### 4. Friendship with God

The supreme example of patronal friendship in ancient sources might be thought to be discovered in passages referring to friendship with God. Surprisingly, however, it is not the patronal but the voluntary, reciprocal elements of the relationship that dominate many of these texts. Some references are too brief for this to be determined, as in some cynic epistles, where the wise person alone is God's friend (Crates Ep. 26, to the Athenians; cf. Diogenes Ep. 10, to Metrocles); one may also compare the fellowship between mortals and deities in the Golden Age (Babrius Fables prol. 13). But *Epictetus addresses the subject more frequently. Heracles had few friends, indeed, no friend closer than God; thus he was an obedient son of God (Epictetus Disc. 2.16.44). One who does not care about circumstances is like a free man and can "look up to heaven as a friend of God" (Epictetus Disc. 3.24.60). As a free man he "is a friend of God," for he chose to obey him willingly (Epictetus Disc. 4.3.9).

### 5. Friendship Contrasted with Servanthood

The contrast between friends and servants was familiar enough in Mediterranean antiquity; a Roman, for example, could describe conquered people as "slaves" but allies as "friends" (Sallust Iug. 102.6). Writers should also draw a contrast between servants and friends of God. Philo declares that God gives priority to his friends (Philo Migr. Abr. 45). Abraham, like Wisdom, is God's friend and not his servant, and those who are his friends are also his only sons (Philo Sobr. 55). Thus John supplements his earlier contrast between servants and children (8.33-35 cf. Gal 4:7) with a contrast between friends and servants in John 15:15. John's context probably explains why Jesus' friends and slaves may also help explicate the context, because only Jesus' friends and slaves may also have all things in common. Under traditional Jewish law, a slave could not inherit, no matter how many goods were left to him, unless the master's will included the slave or granted him freedom. As a servant, he was an obedient slave (cf. Jn 15:15); because wisdom was God's friend rather than servant, God could take Abraham into his confidence, not treating him as a servant (cf. Gen 18:17-18; see Damascus Document; Jacob in CD 3:3-4; see also Moses of Jos. and As. 23:10; cf. Gen. Rab. 69.2). Abraham receives this title especially because of his intimate relationship with God, and to those of his descendants who are not slaves.

## 3. Thematic Analysis Matrices
### Theme: Friendship as Equality
The text argues that while Roman paternal friendship often lacked true equality, the traditional Greek ideal, even when involving benefaction, required a reciprocal good-will. This is evidenced by Diogenes Laertius's definition of friendship as 'an equality of reciprocal good-will' and the concept of a friend as 'another I'.

### Theme: Friendship with God
The document highlights that in ancient sources, friendship with God is characterized by voluntary, reciprocal elements rather than just patronal ones. It cites Epictetus, who describes the wise person as a 'friend of God' because they choose to obey God willingly, and Philo, who notes that God treats Abraham as a friend rather than a servant.

### Theme: Friendship vs. Servanthood
The text contrasts the status of a servant with that of a friend. In the context of John 15:15, it explains that while a servant is obedient, a friend is taken into confidence. This distinction is rooted in the idea that a servant cannot inherit, whereas a friend shares in the master's commonality.
