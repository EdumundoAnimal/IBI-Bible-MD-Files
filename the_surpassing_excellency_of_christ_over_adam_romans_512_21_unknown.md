# The Surpassing Excellency of Christ Over Adam: Romans 5:12-21
**Author:** [[Unknown]]

## 1. Verbatim Source Text
### The Surpassing Excellency of Christ Over Adam: Romans 5:12-21

The Bible tells a story of the history of redemption. It is a cosmic drama wrapped up with a mysterious beginning and a glorious end: Adam and Christ. Adam and Christ in a real sense represent bookends around redemptive history, because what the first Adam lost, the last Adam regained by grace for eternal glory. In redemptive history, there is both similarity and dissimilarity between Adam and Christ. Romans 5:12-21 captures this comparison and contrast in such a way that John Murray calls this passage a "high point of the epistle." For in this comparison of opposites, both Adam and Christ are wrapped in flesh, yet what they accomplished in that flesh is something cosmically different: Adam's sin brought condemnation and death while Christ's righteousness brings justification and life. Properly understanding Romans 5 is crucial to our understanding of sin, because Adam raises questions that can only be answered by seeing the work of Christ as described in Romans 5. The passage is not only important for our understanding of sin but also for our understanding of Paul's pastoral purpose to show that where sin increases, grace increases all the more. Thus the passage unlocks two important mysteries that describe our reality: We understand sin only when we see the work of Christ, and in the work of Christ, grace not only covers sin - it "superabounds". Therefore, I contend that Paul compares and contrasts Adam and Christ in Romans 5:12-21 in order to demonstrate the surpassing excellency of Christ over Adam.

### Comparisons

A surface level reading of Romans 5 shows that Paul compares Adam and Christ as both acting on behalf of a group of people, and in this way, both can be considered federal heads or representatives. While Adam acts as a federal head for all mankind, Christ acts as a federal head for all who are justified. The Romans 5 passage provides expositional evidence for this assertion in what Charles Hodge calls the "main idea" clauses of the passage, verses 12, 18 and 19. We know the "one man" in view in verse 12 is Adam because of his mention in verse 14. The fact that his act brings "death for all" combined with the phrase "because all sinned" demonstrates the public status of Adam, because here we see humanity participating in Adam's representative act. Likewise, Jesus' "one act of righteousness" and "obedience" brings justification and righteousness "for all" and "the many" demonstrating Jesus, like Adam, is a federal head. Though the main idea clauses of verses 12, 18 and 19 give sufficient evidence that both men are federal heads, the typological reference of verse 14 makes this point incontrovertible, because here Adam is described as a "pattern of the one to come." Given the textual evidence in 12, 18 and 19, Paul explains that Adam is a pattern for Christ in the sense that Adam represents humanity. Moreover, this comparative modus operandi governs the rest of the pericope. Therefore, public status shared by Adam and Christ as well as their typological relationship, demonstrates that Paul sets up a parallel comparison of two federal heads. Adam's headship is essential to understanding Paul's argument. However, some argue Adam acts only on his behalf. In an attempt to show that Adam acted merely as an individual, some mitigate humanity's responsibility by claiming that sin is transmitted through imitation rather than propagation. Here again, Adam is portrayed as acting only on his own behalf. This view does not comport with vs.14 wherein death is reigning over those who did not sin in the same way Adam sinned because there was no law. Even without the law, death reigned because of Adam's representative act. This interpretation undermines Paul's intention to compare Adam's union with humanity to Christ's union with the justified and consequently blurs the superabounding grace that serves as a climax for the passage. While the federal status of Adam and Christ demonstrates similarity, Paul's focus on the "one" critical act of each figure in verses 15, 16 and 18 also demonstrate a comparison. In verse 15, the gift (singular) is compared to the trespass. In verse 16, Paul compares Christ's one "gift" to Adam's "one sin." Finally, verse 18 compares "one trespass" of Adam to Christ's "one act of righteousness." While each verse's comparison serves a distinct function, the verses share in common a framework of comparing one act to one act. Paul focuses on the one act rather than many acts so that we may understand our sin in terms of solidarity with Adam, and believer's righteousness in terms of solidarity with Christ. Paul's use of the word "one" in reference to the sin, the trespass, the gift, and the act supports his "governing idea" of solidarity as a framework for comparison. Moreover, this language of "one" act propels the passage closer to its climactic ending which is to show that those who are justified have union with a Christ that is far better than that in Adam. Thus, the similarity in the singularity of both acts reinforces Paul's overarching goal to shine the light on Christ's surpassing excellencies over Adam. Paul not only compares each man's status and single act, but he also compares the extent of each man's act on the people whom they represent. Through the use of three repeated phrases, "reigned", "to all" and "the many", the parallelism shines brightly. Adam's sinful act extends drastic effects by causing death to reign even over those who do not have the law (vs.14). Comparatively, Christ's gift of righteousness "reigns in life" (vs.17). By comparing the "reign" of death and "the gift... in life," the reader experiences the gravity of these acts and their widespread effects. To show the burden of these acts even further, Paul draws a comparison between the great number of recipients affected. "All mankind" is condemned in Adam's act and "all men" are justified in Christ. "The many" die due to Adam's act, but the grace and the gift overflows to the "many" in Christ's one act of righteousness. It is important to see that this text is not explaining a salvation that is for all people regardless of faith in Christ. This parallel language of "reigning," "to all" and "the many" statements helps Paul further compare Adam and Christ and ultimately set up the reader for the climax of Christ's excellencies superabounding. While Paul's purpose to show the surpassing excellency of Christ over Adam is served by comparing federal status, singular acts, and widespread extent, it is further served by the comparison of the effects themselves. Two federal heads are acting here and the extent is drastic, but what exactly is the effect? Murray explains that the effect of the immediate imputation of Adam's sin to humanity is condemnation and death. Verse 19 explains that all men are literally "constituted sinners" by sinning in Adam. The effect of Christ's one act is also forensic in nature, because Paul uses the term "justification" in verse 18. This legal language sets up a similarity that is used for Paul's contrasting purposes. Moreover, the "just as...so" sentence structure of verse 19 signals that Paul is indeed comparing legal effects. Therefore in comparing the actual effects of these two federal heads, we read in Romans 5 a pericope richly laden with parallelism that helps Paul illustrate the contrasts between Adam and Christ.

### Contrasts

In observing comparative features in Romans 5, it is important to note that the similarities simply serve the greater goal by showing dissimilarities between Adam and Christ. Adam is indeed a pattern for Christ, but in no way should we understand this pattern to mean as Adam goes, Jesus goes. The comparison is not absolute. It is for these reasons Murray refers to this pericope as a redemptive historical "dis-analogy" between Adam and Christ. We can see this parallel disjunction at five levels: The contrasted powers, mandates, acts, verdicts and results for Adam and Christ. These disjunctions each serve Paul's purpose to underscore the surpassing excellency of Christ over Adam. Paul, in Romans 5, presents Adam and Christ as possessing profoundly different powers. Adam has no power to give himself life, but is instead given life with a demand placed upon him to obey. Before the fall, Adam has only the power to obey or disobey. Adam chooses to disobey in the "single test" in the garden. Therefore his power is limited to simple obedience or disobedience before God, the life giver (vs. 15). In contrast to Adam, Christ has the power to create and restore life. Christ not only has the power to initiate restoration by coming into the world but he also wields his power to restore what was lost in Adam. Moreover Adam introduces sin before any trespass exists (vs.12). However, the gift Christ brings comes in a context where "many trespasses" exist (vss.16-17), demonstrating the abundance of grace through Christ because it cover more than just Adam's sin; it covers the sins of the many. Therefore Paul shows a dramatic contrast between the powers these men hold in order to show the superior power of Christ over Adam. This pericope not only demonstrates contrasting powers, but also different mandates for the two federal heads. Adam's mission was to demonstrate covenant faithfulness in the context of a fully good creation. Christ, on the other hand, enters into a broken world with a mandate to restore. Furthermore, Adam only understood the personal consequences, not the federal consequences, of his act while Christ possessed full knowledge of the federal effects. His work is for others. Christ's "gift of righteousness" (vs.17) is no accident and its restorative purpose is preconceived in the mind of God. As Murray explains, "the obedience Christ rendered fulfilled the obedience in which Adam failed, but Christ's obedience was not the same in content or demand. Christ was mandated to obey in radically different conditions and required to fulfill radically different demands." In this way, Paul contrasts the vastly different mandates placed upon Adam and Christ. The mandate placed upon Christ is much greater and impossible for a human to accomplish: Adam commits sin and the result is death for all, while Christ bears sin and the climactic demand was to die "for all" who believe (vs. 18). Adam and Christ use their distinct powers and mandates for contrasting acts. "But the gift is not like the trespass" (vs. 15), he writes. This language of "but" and "not" at the beginning of Paul's sentence signals a coming contrast. Paul continues "if many died by the trespass of Adam, how much more did God's grace and the gift that came by the grace overflow to the many!" (vs. 15). The contrast is further intensified by the use of the phrase "how much more". Moreover, grace wins out over the trespass as it "overflows" (περισσεύω). Finally, Paul contrasts the trespass with the gift by describing the "free gift" that is "from God". On the other hand, the trespass is neither free nor from God. The word πάνταϛ in verse 12 is applied to the trespass, but never to the free gift. The gift comes from God alone. Therefore, Paul use of language describes a contrast between the acts themselves. Paul also contrasts God's verdict concerning these acts. We note a forensic contrast in verse 18 between condemnation and justification: "Again, the gift of God is not like the result of the one man's sin: The judgment followed one sin and brought condemnation, but the gift followed many trespasses and brought justification." The free gift here in vs.16 is antithetical to judgment. Condemnation defines the character of the sentence of judgment, and justification defines the character of the free gift. Justification is contrasted to condemnation to show that while Adam's act did have power, Christ's power to justify is infinitely greater and in this sense "the gift of God is not like the trespass" (vs.15). Christ's act is immeasurably greater than that of Adam, for Christ not only "had to suffer the penalty for Adam's sin...but he also had to render to God that perfect obedience to God's law which Adam should have rendered but had failed to perform." Christ not only pays for Adam's individual act, but also for every sinful act that follows, for although one trespass requires condemnation, the gift of God covers not only Adam's representative act but also every trespass that follows (vs. 19). Therefore, Paul contrasts God's verdicts concerning these two acts in order to move us closer to the climax of this pericope where we see Christ's superabounding grace. If verse 18 contrasts the specific verdicts, then verses 20 and 21 contrast the consequent results of these verdicts. These results bring us to the climax where the excellency of Christ over Adam is expressed: God's verdict for Adam's sin is death and God's verdict for Christ's act is life. Paul writes, "The law was added so that the trespass might increase. But where sin increased, grace increased all the more, so that, just as sin reigned in death, so also grace might reign through righteousness to bring eternal life through Jesus Christ our Lord." Here the redemptive historical contrast comes to fruition, for we discover how it is that Christ can bring life where death reigns. Paul uses two different words to describe the results of each act when the law comes. Where sin increases (πλεονάση), grace super-increases (ὑπερεπερίσσευσεν). The result is that where death reigned for all, now life can reign through eternal life for those who believe. Murray gives an apt summary by explaining "With Adam is bound up the entrance of sin into the world and the reign of sin, condemnation and death. With Christ is bound up the entrance of righteousness and the reign of grace, righteousness, justification and life." Therefore in these two climactic verses, Paul's pastoral point ends with the spot light shining brightly on the excellency of Christ, which eclipses Adam's tyranny, for grace reigns and thus surpasses the curse in which all humans are bound.

### Implications of Christ's Excellency

When we observe the sustained parallelism of this pericope combined with the important dissimilarities between Adam and Christ, we begin to see two reasons why it is of vital importance to see the surpassing excellency of Christ over Adam. First, understanding Paul's purpose in this pericope in light of the contrast displayed in Romans 5 is important for our understanding of human sinfulness. Cornelius Plantinga grasps the importance of comparing and contrasting Adam sin to Christ's grace when he writes, "We cannot speak of sin apart from the realities of creation and grace. When we do, we forget the resolve of God to purify believers and all of creation with grace. Human sin is stubborn but not as stubborn as the grace of God and not half so persistent, not half so ready to suffer to win its way." Christ restoring what Adam ruined displays the fact that God did not build sin into creation; sin is abnormal, an intruder and a parasite. It is not the way it is supposed to be. Also, the depth of sin is impossible to understand until the incarnation, life and crucifixion of Jesus Christ. Adam's transgression can only be measured in Christ's work. The drastic disease requires a drastic cure. Yet here in Romans 5 the questions Adam raises about the disease of sin are answered in Christ, the cure. Therefore, to understand the problem of human sinfulness, we must carefully read Romans 5 and understand it in light of Paul's purpose to demonstrate the surpassing excellency of Christ over Adam. Second, grasping Paul's purpose in Romans 5 not only sheds light on sin, but it also gives us a deeper understanding of superabounding grace in redemption history. This pericope touches every part of human history, for it is on these two parallel yet opposing federal heads that the history of humanity turns. In our world, where the gravity of sin seems overwhelming, Romans 5 drops us into the depths of the majesty of Christ to see that it is grace that actually overwhelms! Grace overwhelms us personally, giving us new lives as adopted children. Grace overwhelms the cosmos, mending not only humans but also every part of creation. The free gift of Christ not only saves us from sin but it saves us for a new life and for a new creation. Thus we not only get an understanding of the first Adam's sin in this passage, but more importantly, we get a glimpse of the greatness of the second Adam, Christ, who will bring all of redemptive history to its endpoint with superabounding grace.

## 3. Thematic Analysis Matrices
### Theme: Federal Headship
The document argues that Adam and Christ serve as federal heads or representatives for humanity. Adam represents all mankind in his sin, while Christ represents all who are justified in his righteousness. This is supported by the 'main idea' clauses in Romans 5:12, 18, and 19.

### Theme: Redemptive Historical Dis-analogy
While Paul uses parallelism to compare Adam and Christ, the author emphasizes that these similarities serve to highlight profound dissimilarities. The author cites John Murray's term 'dis-analogy' to explain that while Adam is a pattern for Christ, the comparison is not absolute, and Christ's work is superior.

### Theme: Superabounding Grace
The text identifies the climax of Romans 5 as the demonstration of Christ's grace. It argues that where sin increased (πλεονάση), grace super-increased (ὑπερεπερίσσευσεν), showing that Christ's work not only covers Adam's sin but also the sins of the many, ultimately eclipsing the tyranny of Adam.
