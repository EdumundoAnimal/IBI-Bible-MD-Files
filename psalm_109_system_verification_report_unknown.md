(v. 30) └─ "For He stands at the right hand of the needy" (v. 31) Textual Variants ● Psalm 109:10 — MT vs. LXX / Syriac / 4QPs: ○ Masoretic Text (MT): (wədārəšû mēḥārəbôtêhem – "and let them seek [beg] out of their desolate places"). ○ Septuagint (LXX, Ps 108:10): ekblēthētōsan ek tōn oikopedōn autōn – "let them be cast out of their ruins/dwellings"). This reflects a Vorlage reading (wəgōrəšû, Pual or Niphal of g-r-š, "to drive out/expel"). ○ Dead Sea Scrolls (4QPs^f): Fragmentary, aligns substantially with MT consonantal framework. ○ Evaluation: The MT reading (d-r-š) conveys the image of persistent begging/searching among ruins, whereas the LXX (g-r-š) offers a smoother parallel to (wənôa‘ yānû‘û – "wandering, let them wander"). The MT represents the lectio difficilior and preserves the poetic nuance of destitute searching.

### Grammatical Mechanics

● Volitive Strings (vv. 6–19): Dominated by consecutive jussives (yəhî, tiyyāhaš, ya‘atēq, yəqallēl) and cohortatives. The verbal progression in vv. 6–15 utilizes third-person singular jussives: ○ (hap̄qêḏ ‘ālāw rāšā‘ – Hiphil imperative: "Appoint over him a wicked man!"). ○ (wəśāṭān ya‘ămōḏ ‘al-yəmînô – imperfect/jussive with copulative: "and let an adversary stand at his right hand"). ○ (bəhiššāpəṭô yēṣē rāšā‘ – Preposition bə + Niphal infinitive construct + 3ms suffix: "In his being judged, let him go out guilty/condemned"). ○ (ûtəp̄illātô tihyeh laḥăṭā’āh – imperfect 3fs functioning as jussive: "and let his prayer become sin!"). ● Syntactic Marker of Direct Speech vs. Continuous Imprecation: ○ A central syntactic debate centers on whether vv. 6–19 represent David's prayer against his enemies, or David quoting the curses spoken against him by his enemies (the "Quotation Hypothesis"). ○ Grammatical assessment: Hebrew poetry regularly employs ellipsis of (āmar, "saying") (e.g., Ps 2:2–3; Ps 22:8–9). In v. 5, the psalmist concludes: "they laid upon me evil for good, and hatred for my love, [saying]: 'Appoint over him a wicked man...'" ○ Counter-syntax: In v. 20, the MT uses the plural: "This is the reward of my accusers (śōṭənay, plural construct) from Yahweh, and of those who speak evil against my soul." If vv. 6–19 are a quotation of the singular enemy, v. 20 sums up the multiple conspirators. Both interpretations are grammatically plausible; however, the canonical integration (Acts 1:20) treats the curses as the righteous, inspired judgment of the Davidic King upon the treacherous covenant-breaker. ● The Chiastic Inclusio of the "Right Hand" (yāmîn): ○ v. 6b: (wəśāṭān ya‘ămōḏ ‘al-yəmînô – "let an accuser stand at his right hand"). ○ v. 31b: (kî-ya‘ămōḏ liymîn ’eḇyôn – "for He stands at the right hand of the needy"). ○ The structural syntax hinges on this antithetical judicial placement: the accuser stands at the right hand of the wicked to prosecute him to destruction, while Yahweh stands at the right hand of the afflicted to deliver his life from those who pass capital sentence.

## 3. Thematic Analysis Matrices
### Theme: Covenant Theodicy
The document argues that Psalm 109 is not an expression of personal malice but a liturgical appeal to the Supreme Court of Heaven to enforce Mosaic covenant sanctions (lex talionis) against those who have committed treason against God's Anointed.

### Theme: Typological-Christological Vindication
The psalm is interpreted as a prophetic type fulfilled in the life and passion of Jesus Christ, where the innocent Davidic King is betrayed by a Judas-like figure, and the curses of the law are absorbed by Christ on the Cross.

### Theme: The Eschatological Overthrow of the Accuser
The text posits that the 'accuser' (śāṭān) mentioned in the psalm finds its ultimate historical instantiation in Judas Iscariot and its final eschatological end in the judgment of the cosmic Accuser at the Parousia.
