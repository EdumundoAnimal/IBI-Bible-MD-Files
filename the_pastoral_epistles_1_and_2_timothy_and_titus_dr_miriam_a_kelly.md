# The Pastoral Epistles: 1 and 2 Timothy and Titus
**Author:** [[Dr. Miriam A Kelly]]

## 1. Verbatim Source Text
### Introduction

The name 'Pastoral Epistles' was first attributed to Paul's letters to Timothy and Titus by D.N. Berdot in 1703, and popularised by the book of that name written by Paul Anton in 1726. (Cf. Walter W. Wessel, 'Titus' in Exploring NT Backgrounds), 43. And the name has stuck ever since. Although Philemon is commonly thought of as the most personal of Paul's letters, it is also addressed to the church that meets in Philemon's house. This means that 1 and 2 Timothy and Titus are unique because they are written to individuals. Both Tim and Titus are an integral part of the work in the 'new churches' planted by the missionaries. Technically, 1 Timothy and Titus have more in common with each other as pastoral exhortations than does 2 Timothy, Paul appears to be facing his own death and knows that his time is short (2 Tim 4:6-8). It is important to read the Pastorals in the light of Paul's circumstances. When he penned 1 Tim and Titus he was free to move above and minister. However, by the Paul expresses himself. If 2 Tim is read as a prison epistle it is much easier to grasp the difference.

### Authorship

Until the rise of modern scholarship the authorship of the Pastoral Epistles was unquestioned. However, since the advent of German higher criticism, many critical scholars have doubted that Paul wrote these letters. But since the alternatives to the traditional view raise more questions than solutions, the traditional view of authorship is maintained here.

### Timothy the Man

Timothy is best known to us through his missionary travels with the Apostle. From early in Paul's second missionary journey, Timothy a native of Lystra, becomes part of the evangelistic team (cf. Acts 16). Timothy, who participated in the establishment of the churches in Macedonia, was often left to encourage new believers where Paul had been forced to leave a locality. He also accompanied Paul during his imprisonment and acted as his envoy to Philippi and elsewhere (Col 1:1; Phile 1). When Paul writes to Timothy he is Paul's representative at Ephesus (1 Tim 1:3; 2 Tim 4:12) not the overseer of the church, which already had its own elders (Acts 2:17,28). There are some significant indications in Paul's letters to Timothy as to the character of the man. He was young and at times unsure, but deeply committed to the Gospel and to Paul, his spiritual father and mentor. The apostle had total confidence in Timothy as his representative and friend. This love for Timothy is clear from the fact that it is to this man that Paul calls on in his hour of need (2 Tim 1:4; 4:12,21).

### Possible Dates of Paul's Pastoral Epistles:

1 Timothy A.D. 62: Written from Macedonia
Titus A.D. 63: Written from Ephesus, some scholars place it later about A.D.66
2 Timothy A.D.64 or a little later about A.D.67: Written from Rome, shortly before Paul's death?

### 2 Timothy

Background
When Paul wrote this letter he was imprisoned at Rome although it is not long since ha had been at Troas, Corinth and Miletus (2 Tim 4:12,20). During the first Roman imprisonment Paul was living in his own house and was free to receive friends, but here he is manacled and in anticipation of his death (2:9; 4:6). Many of his friends have abandoned him (1:15) and at his first defence no one wished to be associated with him or testify in his favour (4:16). It appears that Paul's own defence was sufficient to acquit him but he was not released. The timing of the letter and Paul's imprisonment very likely coincides with Nero's declaration of Christianity as a religio illicita (illegal religion) following the great fire at Rome in A.D. 64.

Occasion
The occasion for this letter to Timothy is clearly that of Paul's concern for Timothy. Paul wishes to encourage Timothy not to give up because of his own imprisonment and imminent death since he needed to continue to testify to the power of the Gospel (1:8). Paul knows that Timothy would have been deeply troubled about his friend and mentor and wishes to assure him that this was all part of being a soldier for Christ (2:3). There is no need to be ashamed, as a believer, because Christ was in control and Timothy must never give up. Death did not have the final say as Christ had overcome death (1:10).
Not only did Paul wish to encourage Timothy, he had the deepest desire to see him and be encouraged by his protégé (1:4). It is clear that Paul felt abandoned by many of his closest friends and in the face of his execution wished to see Timothy once more.

### Possible Outline of 1 Timothy:

-Warning regarding riches and the need for pastoral priorities
Final charge: (6:20-21)

### Titus

Titus the Man
Titus is first mentioned in Gal 2:1, where he is associated with Paul and his journey to Jerusalem. There is no mention made of him anywhere in Acts, although he appears in the Corinthian letters and must have been known to Luke (cf. 2 Cor 2:13). Titus, who had been part of the missionary team for many years, is known as Paul's delegate to Corinth on several occasions (2 Cor 7:6-7; 8:6). The letter to Titus is sent while latter is on an errand for Paul to the Island of Crete (Tit 1:5).

Background
The origin of the church in Crete is unknown. Paul passed through there on his way to Rome (acts 27:21) and there were Jews from Crete in Jerusalem on the day of Pentecost (acts 2:11). Since there is no mention of any evangelistic event during Paul's voyage it is most likely that the church had been founded by converted Jews. This would make sense since a number of the difficulties among the believers appear to be linked to the influence of the Judaizers (1:14; 3:9). The situation described in this letter does not fit into the missionary journeys described in Acts and must have taken place after the first detention in Rome but clearly before the final imprisonment described in Second Timothy.

Occasion
Paul had been in Crete with Titus whom he left to establish the believers by means of doctrinal instruction and to appoint elders in every town (1:5). The other main concern of Paul was the organisation of the churches.

Content
The pastoral content of the letter is mostly concerned with the setting in place of eldership and the nature of the teaching to be given to the various groups within the church. It includes that fact that elders must be capable of refuting wrong doctrine and practice.
The qualifications for eldership are elaborated upon (1:6-9; 1 Tim 3). Elders must be capable of teaching sound doctrine and refuting falsehood. There are moral attributes, as well as teaching abilities necessary for this ministry. Cretans appear to have a much food for thought in this letter. (Carson and Moo, in Introduction to the NT, 380, cite David Barrett who estimates that there are as many as 300,000 Christian martyrs each year in our time, more than during the persecution of the Roman state).
It is generally thought that verses 11-13 are part of an early Christian hymn. It certainly fits with the teaching of Jesus in Mt. 10:33 and needs to be understood in a setting where there was a very real threat of capital punishment for those who were believers.

### To Each Its Own Meaning: Traditional Methods of Biblical Criticism

As an objective approach to literature 'criticism' means 'evaluation', biblical criticism involves the exercising of judgement about the Bible. 'When scholarly judgement is applied to the genuineness of the biblical text, it is classified as 'higher' or 'historical' criticism. This judgement is applied to the date of the text, its literary style and structure, its literary form, its historicity, its sources, and its authorship. When scholarly judgement is applied to the authenticity of the biblical text, it is classified as 'lower' or 'textual' criticism.'

Textual criticism (lower criticism) See also p 189-192 in Soulen
Textual criticism, commonly known in the past as 'lower' criticism, is the science that compares all known manuscripts of a given work in an effort to trace the history of variations within the text so as to discover its original form. TC is, therefore, of special significance to the interpreter in at least three ways: 1: It helps to determine the authentic words of an author. The first question the exegete asks is, What does the text say? Before he/she asks, What does it mean? 2: The majority of Christians have access to the NT only in translation, and the basic consideration in choosing a translation is its accuracy in representing the original text of the author. A translator's first concern must be that he/she is translating the actual words of the author before they decide what those words mean. 3: A knowledge of the history of textual variation will also help the interpreter to see how a passage was understood during the early history of the church. In many instances variant readings are a reflection of a scribe's or a church's theological interests, and sometimes such changes put one in direct contact with historical exegesis.
The need for NT textual criticism results from a combination of three factors: 1: The originals, probably written on papyrus scrolls, have all perished. 2: For over 1,400 years the NT was copied by hand, and the copyists (scribes) made errors, as well as times intentionally altering (probably with the idea of 'correcting') the text. Such errors and alterations survived in various ways, with a basic tendency to accumulate (scribes seldom left anything out, lest they omit something inspired). 3: There are now extant, in whole or in part, 5,338 Greek MSS, as well as hundreds of copies of ancient translations (not counting over 8,000 copies of the Latin Vulgate), plus the evidence from the citations of the NT in the writings of the early church fathers.

### Form Criticism (Formgeschichte), which literally means 'history of form' See also p 61-64 in Soulen

Martin Dibelius used it first in his work From (Oral) Tradition to Gospel. He applied the form-critical method used by Hermann Gunkel in his OT studies to the Synoptic Gospels. FC, instead of classifying material by source, it does it by the nature of the material. FC approaches the study of the New Testament literature in terms of oral or written units or forms, such as stories, sayings and parables. Interest here is in the social setting, or Sitz im Leben, in which the forms took shape. This involves the application of Hermann Gunkel's Old Testament method to the New Testament. Three of his pupils worked on the Gospels: K.L. Schmidt, M. Dibelius and R. Bultmann. Dibelius thought that it was the church's need for sermonic material, which was the Sitz im Leben that had created or shaped and determined the content of the NT traditions. It seeks to trace the historical situation of the Gospels.

### Tradition-Historical Criticism (Traditionsgeschichte) See also p 198-200 in Soulen

TC, or traditio-historical criticism, is primarily concerned with the history of traditions before they were recorded into writing and incorporated into literary sources. It studies the history of oral traditions during their time of transmission. Form criticism is closely aligned to TC. 'In NT studies, T-HC is concerned with the development of tradition in the pre-Pauline period, ca. A.D. 30-50. It has dealt especially with the oldest liturgical fragments, hymns, juridical formulas etc., within the context of baptism, the Eucharist, catechesis, proclamations etc.'

### Redaction Criticism (Redaktionsgeschichte) See also p 158-160 in Soulen

A redactor is 'one who arranges, revises, edits or otherwise shapes oral and literary materials into a final composition'. (R.N. Soulen, Handbook of B.C.). RC is a method of biblical criticism, which seeks to ascertain the theological perspectives of a biblical writer by analysing the editorial (redactional) and compositional techniques and interpretations employed by him in shaping and framing the written and/or oral traditions at hand (Lk. 1:1-4). Whereas FC stressed the sociological setting of the NT traditions, RC related to a literary setting, for example 'the setting in the Gospel'. (Sitz im Evalgelium).

### Overturning The Tradition

In part three, Mc Kenzie and Haynes, the editors of To Each Its Own Meaning overview modern types of biblical criticisms and for the purpose of this module you only need to be aware of these. This book is in the library, along with Soulen and Soulen's book, Handbook of Biblical Criticism.
Structural Criticism: Pages 183-200, See also p 180-183 in Soulen
Narrative Criticism: 201-229, See also p 119-120 in Soulen
Reader-Response Criticism: 230-252, See also p 156-157 in Soulen
Refers to a literary approach that is centrally concerned with the reader and the process of reading rather than with the author or the text as a self-contained unity. Although similar in many respects to the movement known as reception Theory, from which it in part derives, reader-response criticism is a much more pluralistic phenomenon that lacks a single focused methodology. Nevertheless, practitioners generally subscribe to two key premises. First, the meaning of a literary text does not reside 'within' the text as a self-contained unity but is actualised or created by the interaction of the reader and the text. Literature is like a performative art, analogous to the performance of a musical work or the staging of a drama. Second (and accordingly), the meaning of a text can differ from reader to reader and indeed from 'performance' to 'performance', as different readers perform the text in different circumstances to different ends. While RRC stems from general literary theory, it has become increasingly prominent in biblical studies in the last two decades. Its insights have been applied to the study of a variety of biblical genres, from Parables to Gospels to the biblical canon as a whole.

## 3. Thematic Analysis Matrices
### Theme: Pastoral Authority and Mentorship
The text highlights the unique relationship between Paul and his protégés, Timothy and Titus. Paul acts as a spiritual father and mentor, entrusting them with the responsibility of organizing churches and appointing elders, while maintaining his own authority as an apostle.

### Theme: Historical Context of Imprisonment
The document argues that the timing of the Pastoral Epistles, particularly 2 Timothy, is crucial for understanding the tone of the writing. Paul's situation as a prisoner in Rome, facing potential execution under Nero, shifts the tone from the instructional nature of 1 Timothy and Titus to a more personal, urgent exhortation.

### Theme: Biblical Criticism Methodology
The text provides a taxonomy of biblical criticism, distinguishing between 'higher' (historical/literary) and 'lower' (textual) criticism. It emphasizes that these methods are tools for evaluation, aiming to understand the date, authorship, and original form of the biblical text.
