# Introduction to Biblical Interpretation
**Author:** [[Dr. William W. Klein, Dr. Craig L. Blomberg, Dr. Robert L. Hubbard, Jr.]]

## 1. Cleaned Document Text
The document serves as a comprehensive guide to biblical hermeneutics. It introduces the fundamental need for interpretation, explaining that while the Bible is God's word, it is conveyed through human means in diverse historical, cultural, and linguistic contexts, necessitating a systematic approach. The text covers the role of the interpreter, the nature of communication, and the complex challenge of overcoming historical and cultural distance. It also provides a historical survey of interpretation, spanning Jewish interpretation, the Apostolic period, the Patristic era, the Middle Ages, and the Reformation, outlining how various interpretive strategies like allegory, literalism, and typology have been utilized throughout history to bridge the gap between ancient texts and contemporary readers.

## 3. Thematic Analysis Matrices
### Theme: Hermeneutical Necessity
Biblical interpretation is required because the Bible contains God's message conveyed in diverse human languages, genres, and historical settings, which creates a gap between the original context and modern readers.

### Theme: The Role of the Interpreter
Interpreters are not objective observers; they bring their own presuppositions and agendas to the text, which must be acknowledged and managed to ensure a faithful understanding of the intended meaning.

### Theme: Historical and Cultural Distance
A significant challenge in biblical interpretation involves overcoming the vast differences in time, language, culture, and geography that separate the original audience from the modern reader.

### Theme: Evolution of Interpretive Methods
The history of interpretation shows a shift from early Jewish allegorical methods and the early church's use of typology to the systematic scholastic approach of the Middle Ages and the Reformation's emphasis on the literal sense.
