## 1. Verbatim Source Text
### PHASE 1: THE EXEGETICAL NETWORK (The First-Century Chair)

1. Linguistic & Syntax Report Textual Variants (BHS, LXX, Qumran Apparatus) ● Superscription (v. 1a): The Masoretic Text (MT) reads שִׁיר לַמַּעֲלוֹת (šîr lamma‘alôt, "A song for the ascents"), uniquely utilizing the preposition lam- (לַ) with the definite article, in contrast to the other fourteen Songs of Ascents (Psalms 120, 122–134), which employ the construct state שִׁיר הַמַּעֲלוֹת (šîr hamma‘alôt, "A song of the ascents"). The Septuagint (LXX) harmonizes this distinction to ᾠδὴ τῶν ἀναβαθμῶν (ōidē tōn anabathmōn), translating all fifteen identically. The 4QPs^e (4Q87) and 11QPs^a (11Q5) manuscripts from the Dead Sea Scrolls confirm the antiquity of the MT reading lamma‘alôt, preserving the unique dative/directional nuance ("for the journeys upward"). ● Verse 1b: The interrogative מֵאַיִן יָבֹא עֶזְרִי (mē’ayin yāvô’ ‘ezrî, "From where does my help come?") is translated in the Targum as a direct question, whereas early Latin witnesses (Old Latin, pre-Jerome) occasionally mistranslated the particle as a relative conjunction (unde veniet auxilium mihi, read as "whence my help shall come"). The Syriac Peshitta and Jerome's Juxta Hebraicos confirm the interrogative nature of מֵאַיִן (mē’ayin), establishing that verse 1b functions as a rhetorical/existential inquiry rather than a relative clause. ● Verse 3a: The jussive negation אַל-יִתֵּן (al-yittēn, "May He not allow...") is rendered by the LXX as μὴ δῴης / μὴ δῷ (mē dōiēs / mē dō, jussive subjunctive), preserving the cohortative/optative tension of the Hebrew against late medieval attempts to flatten it into an absolute indicative future (which would require לֹא, lō’). Grammatical & Syntactical Mechanics אֶשָּׂא עֵינַי אֶל-הֶהָרִים מֵאַיִן יָבֹא עֶזְרִי (Verse 1): ○ אֶשָּׂא (eśśā’): Qal imperfect 1st person common singular from נָשָׂא (nāśā’), functioning as a cohortative of resolve ("I will lift up / Let me lift up"). ○ הֶהָרִים (hehārîm): Noun masculine plural with definite article. The direction-oriented preposition אֶל (el) denotes spatial orientation toward the elevation of the Judean ridge or the hill shrines (bāmôt). ○ מֵאַיִן (mē’ayin): Interrogative adverb of source ("From whence?"). ○ יָבֹא (yāvô’): Qal imperfect 3rd person masculine singular from בּוֹא (bô’), signifying continuous or emerging action.

### Lexical Profiles

● שָׁמַר (šāmar, HALOT 1565–1568; DCH 8:435–442): Occurs six times in this eight-verse psalm (vv. 3, 4, 5, 7a, 7b, 8) as participle שֹׁמֵר (šōmēr) and finite verb יִשְׁמֹר (yišmōr). In biblical Hebrew, šāmar extends beyond passive observation; it denotes active military guarding, covenantal guardianship, custodial preservation, and defensive escort. Its structural dominance renders it the definitive theological Leitmotiv of the text. ● עֵזֶר (ēzer, HALOT 811–812): Noun denoting active intervention, rescue, and succor. In the Old Testament, God is predominantly the subject of ‘ēzer when human strength is completely exhausted (e.g., Exod 18:4; Deut 33:29; Ps 33:20). It does not denote a subordinate assistant, but an indispensable, superior deliverer. ● צֵל (ṣēl, HALOT 1024–1025): Shadow, shade. In Ancient Near Eastern (ANE) royal and cosmic literature, ṣēl is the protective sphere cast by a monarch, temple, or sovereign deity, shielding the client from hostile environmental and supernatural assaults (cf. Num 14:9; Isa 30:2; Hos 14:7). ● רַגְלְךָ לַמּוֹט (raḡləḵā lammôṭ, HALOT 556): Idiomatic expression for catastrophic ruin, total moral collapse, or physical destruction along perilous mountain ravines (cf. Ps 38:16; 66:9; 94:18).

## 3. Thematic Analysis Matrices
### Theme: Covenantal Preservation (Shamar)
The document identifies 'shamar' as the definitive theological Leitmotiv of Psalm 121, denoting active military guarding, covenantal guardianship, and custodial preservation rather than passive observation.

### Theme: Cosmic Demythologization
The psalm is presented as a polemic against Ancient Near Eastern astral deities (Sun/Shamash, Moon/Yarikh), asserting that Yahweh, as the Creator, holds absolute sovereignty over the forces of nature that were historically feared as disease-inducing or malevolent.

### Theme: Theodicy and Suffering
The report resolves the tension between the promise of preservation and the reality of suffering by defining 'evil' (ra') as that which causes the ultimate shipwreck of faith, arguing that God preserves the believer's 'nephesh' (soul/true life) through suffering rather than granting exemption from it.
