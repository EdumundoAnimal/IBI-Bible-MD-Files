# Christian Discipleship Manual
**Author:** [[Pastor Brian Gingles]]

## 1. Verbatim Source Text
### 1. What is a Disciple?

The New Testament speaks about Christian “disciples” rather than “converts”. What is the difference? When you convert to Christ you accept Him as your personal Saviour; this is the beginning of your new life in Him and is sometimes referred to as “new birth”, or being “born again”. As Colossians 2:13-14 puts it: “And you, who were dead in trespasses and the uncircumcision of your flesh, God made alive together with Him, having forgiven us all our trespasses, having cancelled the bond which stood against us with its legal demands; this He set aside, nailing it to the cross.” (RSV) Conversion - new birth - is just the beginning of your new life in Christ. This is a life of intimacy with God, your Heavenly Father, based on the atoning work of Christ and empowered and guided daily by the Holy Spirit. This is Good News! If conversion is genuine - and Christ has become your Lord as well as your Saviour - then your new life will endure, kept by God’s power. You will become a disciple. The baby Christian will become a mature Christian, equipped to help others. So what is a disciple? A Christian disciple is someone who listens to, and submits to their Lord and Saviour; they will constantly sustain a humble attitude and a desire to learn and obey the truth of God, as revealed in His Word, the Bible. You, as a true disciple, will also acknowledge your need for revelation and transformation, which can only be achieved with the help of the Holy Spirit.

### Some Benefits of Being a Disciple

As a disciple who is “in Christ” you now have an intimate relationship with God, who has become your Heavenly Father (unlike human fathers, He will never let you down). The Holy Spirit will lead you into “all truth” (John 16:13) when you decide to put Christ first in your life at all times. The true disciple will recognize that Christianity is not a sprint - it is a marathon (or, more accurately, a relay race). The genuine disciple will “endure to the end” (Matthew 24:13) regardless of temptations, trials, temporary failures and doubts. He will learn to trust his Heavenly Father completely in all situations. The true disciple will seek God’s deliverance and healing, and will avoid sin. You, as a true disciple, will seek to be changed from within, in order to bear an increasing crop of the “fruit of the Spirit” in your character and behaviour (Galatians 5:22-24). You will seek to love God with all your being and love your neighbour by selfless acts of service (Luke 10:27). The true disciple will seek to spread the Good News that Jesus Christ is the Messiah.

### Two Parables

There are two well known parables (earthly stories with heavenly meanings) that illustrate the need to be a true disciple of Christ. The first is the Parable of the Sower (Luke 8:1-15), in which the good seeds (God’s words) fall on three very different kinds of soil (representing different heart conditions). One heart condition is characterized as a hard path - this is a heart that hardens itself when it hears about Jesus. Another heart condition is portrayed as ground that initially receives the seed, which then begins to grow but is gradually choked by weeds and dies. The third heart condition is represented as good soil that has been tilled and cleared of weeds, from which a harvest is produced (note that the seed is good in all three cases: it is the condition of the soil - the heart responses of those who hear God’s words - which differ). The idea of harvest is continued in our second parable, the Parable of The Vineyard (John 15:1-11). In this parable, the branch (the disciple) is connected to the stem of the vine (Jesus). This intimate, sustaining relationship naturally produces an abundant harvest. Read the parable and think about its meaning for you. These two parables of harvest portray the life of the true disciple as one of intimacy, dependence and divine empowerment - all to produce a wonderful harvest for God’s glory. By now, if you have heeded the lessons of these parables, you will already be bearing spiritual fruit.

### Trust, Forgiveness and Death to Self

In all of this you will develop an absolute dependence on, and trust in, all that Jesus has accomplished for you. Remember, we are what we are only because of the Lord’s grace. We do not deserve any of it and we must be patient with ourselves, as the Lord changes us a bit at a time. What God seeks from all of us is our faith, our trust and our humble obedience. So, our “flesh” (our carnal, sinful nature) needs to be “crucified daily” (Matthew 16:24-26) because faith, trust and obedience will never come from that carnal nature that we are putting to death, and is already dead in Christ, by faith. It is very important for the disciple to learn not to live with guilt and condemnation. There is a car bumper sticker in the States that says, “Be patient with me: God hasn’t finished with me yet.” Your salvation relies on what Jesus did and you have an imputed righteousness (one given to you freely), so Father God sees you clothed in Jesus. As Paul puts it in Romans 8:1: “There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus.” (RSV) God understands that you are not perfect but He will help you to change so that you gradually become more mature and righteous: more like Jesus.

### The Cost of Discipleship

Salvation is free (because it is based on grace) but discipleship costs the genuine Christian disciple everything. Jesus made this clear when speaking to His earliest followers: “Then Jesus told His disciples, ‘If any man would come after me let him deny himself and take up his cross and follow me. For whoever would save his life will lose it, and whoever loses his life for my sake will find it’.” Matthew 16:24-25 (RSV) In Romans 12:1-2 Paul appeals to the church: “I appeal to you therefore, brethren, by the mercies of God, to present your bodies as a living sacrifice, holy and acceptable to God, which is your spiritual worship. Do not be conformed to this world but be transformed by the renewal of your mind, that you may prove what is the will of God, what is good and acceptable and perfect.” (RSV) A person who is a living sacrifice is someone who has died to all selfish desires, surrendering every personal need to the will of God and seeking always to obey and please Him. God will never accept second place, so you must deal with any “idols” that threaten to take God’s place in your life. The symbolism of taking up your personal cross implies that you must embrace fully the implications of the price Jesus was prepared to pay for you. As Christ’s disciples, can you pay anything less for Him than He paid for you? This love for God may require the ultimate sacrifice; as Paul well knew: “that I may know Him and the power of His resurrection, and may share His sufferings, becoming like Him in His death” Philippians 3:10 (RSV) It is worth remembering that Paul was eventually martyred in Rome. Not every disciple will be martyred but disciples ought to love God enough to be willing to pay any price for Him. This sacrificial, devoted attitude and your enlightened acknowledgement of what the blood of Christ has won for you give you ultimate victory over Satan: “And they [Christian disciples in the last days] have conquered him [Satan] by the blood of the Lamb and by the word of their testimony, for they loved not their lives even unto death.” Revelation 12:11 (RSV)

## 3. Thematic Analysis Matrices
### Theme: Conversion vs. Discipleship
The text distinguishes between the initial act of conversion (new birth) and the ongoing life of a disciple. Conversion is the beginning, while discipleship is the lifelong process of submission, learning, and transformation through the Holy Spirit.

### Theme: The Cost of Discipleship
Discipleship is presented as a costly endeavor requiring the denial of self, the 'crucifying' of the flesh daily, and the willingness to offer one's life as a 'living sacrifice' to God, mirroring the sacrifice of Christ.

### Theme: Justification and Assurance
The author emphasizes that salvation is based on grace and imputed righteousness, freeing the believer from guilt and condemnation, which allows for a life of growth and maturity rather than performance-based religion.
