# History of Interpretation and Biblical Hermeneutics
**Author:** [[Unknown]]

## 1. Verbatim Source Text
### History of Interpretation, part two...

Blessed Lord, who caused all holy Scriptures to be written for our learning: Grant me so to hear them, read, mark, learn, and inwardly digest them, that I may embrace and ever hold fast the blessed hope of everlasting life which you have given us in our Savior Jesus Christ; who lives and reigns with you and the Holy Spirit, one God, forever and ever. Amen.

### Review - Intro to Hermeneutics

Definition of. Need for. Challenges to. Approach for class.

### History of Interpretation: review...

Interpretation within the OT. Intertestamental period. NT Period. Patristic period. Medieval period.

### OT Interpretation: review

Later OT books draw on previous writings (Chronicles, Isaiah, etc). Toward the end of the OT era, people need help understanding Scripture because of language and cultural issues (Ezra & Nehemiah). Thus - the interpretive process has begun...

### Intertestamental period: review

Translations appear - they make interpretive decisions (eg. Targum). First commentaries: people begin to retell biblical stories and embellish them to make heroes look better, enemies look worse (eg. Jubilees). Some begin to interpret Scripture apologetically, so that it makes sense to others outside the faith (eg. Philo). People begin to see events from their own time when reading Scripture (eg. Qumran).

### Reformation (1500-1650 CE)

Erasmus (early 1500s) rediscovers original Greek and Hebrew - stranglehold of Vulgate begins to diminish; authority of church begins to be questioned. Growing frustration with allegorical method.

### Luther

Luther: sola scriptura: only Scripture has divine authority for Christians - broke with long standing traditional interpretation. Scripture itself is its own best interpreter. Rejected allegory, focused on plain sense of historical understanding. Luther: Rejected allegory, but still practised typology (Christological reading of OT). Stressed subjective element to interpretation: role of the Holy Spirit. Focus on 'tradition' had limited this. Gave the Bible back to the common people - first common German translation.

### Calvin

See Calvin's Commentaries in library - wrote volumes of commentaries, something that hadn't been done for some time. Calvin: With Luther... rejected allegory for literal interpretation. upheld sola scriptura. Realised subjective element, 'internal witness of the HS', which doesn't inspire but confirms. Translated and wrote commentaries in French for Swiss people.

### The Post-Reformation period (1650-1800 CE)

Originally, rationalism was used by people to bolster faith; however, this would eventually do the opposite. Neil: 'Rationalism was not a system of beliefs antagonistic to Christianity, but an attitude of mind which assumed that in all matters of religion reason is supreme.' Examples: Thomas Hobbes; Bernard Spinoza. Spinoza (who was Jewish) argued that the Bible should be read like any other book, using the normal rules of historical investigation. Example: Scriptural claims to God's direct intervention in history are simply common Jewish ways of speaking, not actual revelation. Spinoza subjected Scripture to the authority of the human mind rather than the other way around.

### The Post-Reformation period (1650-1800 CE): review

Biblical interpretation goes in two directions: Pietism carries on the ideas of the Reformation, seeks to read the Bible for personal growth and development. Rationalism carries on the ideas of the Renaissance, and puts human reason as the highest authority when reading Scripture.

### The Modern period (1800-)

People in churches continue to follow the tradition of the pietists, reading the Bible devotionally for personal spiritual growth. Elsewhere, confidence in the scientific method continues to grow - the 'scientific method' becomes the standard way to approach all research, including history, literary studies, etc. Hegel (philosophy), Darwin (biology), etc. The Bible did not escape these changes: Academic scholars started approaching the Bible with objective, scientific methods. This led to what became known as the 'historical-critical method' of interpretation. Human reason is needed above all else. Rationalistic worldview - explain things in light of natural laws.

### Twentieth century (1900s):

Barth - brought back theological interpretation: Criticised the mistakes of liberalism, sought a return to Reformation thinking. Emphasised authority of Scripture and need for personal relationship in interpretation. But, also noted the importance of historical-critical questions and what had been learned in the previous generation. Barth and those who followed him (e.g. Brevard Childs) sought to combine what was happening in the church and in academics: Use the tools of the 'scientific method', but do so to read the Bible as Scripture, as God's word. Stranglehold of 'historical-critical method' of interpretation begins to loosen.

### Postmodernity

Post WW II: Birth of other postmodern interpretations: Literary criticism, structuralism, deconstruction. Feminist, liberation, post-colonial. With the 'historical-critical method' losing its place of preeminence, no one method has risen to replace it (in academic settings). Thus biblical studies is at a place of extreme methodological pluralism. A 'theological approach' to interpretation is just one option available to people today who wish to study the Bible. Meanwhile, people in the church have continued to read Scripture devotionally, as they always have, often unaware of what has been happening in the academic world of interpretation.

### Twentieth century (1900s)/postmodernity - review:

After the two world wars there is a resurgence in theological approaches to reading Scripture in academics, not just the church (Barth). 'Historical-critical method' eventually loses its stranglehold on biblical studies. A plurality of approaches to reading the Bible spring up in its place: literary criticism, deconstruction, feminist criticism, etc., alongside theological readings.

### Reformation (1500-1650 CE): review

Move to reject allegory and toward literal, historical interpretation. Undermines tradition and apostolic succession approach to interpretation. Scripture seen as highest authority - sola scriptura. Lots of commentaries written on biblical text - something that hadn't been done in a long time.

### The Modern period (1800-): review

Divide between devotional reading in the church and scientific reading in the Universities continues. Scientific method gives birth to the 'historical-critical' method of interpretation. This method brackets out questions of faith and meaning and focuses on issues of historical background, source documents, etc.

### History of Interpretation: review...

Recurring issues: The tension between eternal significance and historical particularity. Overcoming the obstacles of distance. The question of contextualisation, or application in the here and now. Where does that leave us? What do we do with all that history, and where do we place ourselves? I feel we take the best of both the ancient and modern methods...

### Theological Interpretation

We want to use the tools history has passed down to us, but we do so with the awareness that our allegiance is to God, not to human reason, and that the purpose of our interpretation is for edification and instruction.

### Example: Matt. 9:9-13

What does it mean to read Matt 9:9-13 in light of this? What is the historical context? What has the historical-critical method shown us about this text? What are the theological implications of the text? What can we learn from it that will help us to become more like our God? Historical Context: What historical dimensions do we need to be aware of? persons, places, things, cultural issues, customs... Pharisees? Tax collectors? Sinners? Meals? The NT doesn't tell us a lot about these things - it assumes that the reader knows. So we have to do some historical digging... Historical Context: Judea is under Roman rule; they've been under foreign rule for over 600 years (Assyrians, Babylonians, Persians, Greeks, Romans). Different ideas as to why this was this case and what should be done about it: Fight back - zealots. Make the most of it - Sadducees. Withdraw - Essenes. Be holy and righteous - Pharisees.

### Pharisees

Weren't professional scholars or priests; volunteer lay people who devoted themselves to studying the Scriptures and the law. Holy and pious people - encouraged people to be faithful and follow God. Thought God's blessing would come if people were faithful to law and purely holy. Of all the groups in the NT era, Jesus was probably closest in beliefs and theology to the Pharisees. The Pharisees felt that there were two types of people who were holding back God's blessing, that were essentially responsible for the state of the Jewish people: Those who collaborated with Rome. Those who didn't observe God's laws, and thus were unholy people.

### Matt. 9:9-13 - literary context

Literary Context: What literary elements do we need to be aware of? What is the context - i.e. what comes before and after this passage? Are there other passages of Scripture which shed light on this text? 9:1-8 - Jesus heals a paralytic/forgives sins: sinners: note relation to previous paragraph where Jesus forgives sins; now he eats with sinners. 9:14-17 - Jesus questioned about fasting: Another area where Jesus breaks with tradition. What does this mean? Jesus has been subverting the status quo and questioning tradition in the surrounding narratives as well. 'I desire mercy...' - this is a quote from Hos. 6:6 - Note that Jesus frequently addresses the concerns of the religious establishment with scripture. It's not that they don't know scripture, they are the experts. It's that they don't truly understand it. Jesus quoting Scripture at the Pharisees is a bit of a slap in the face; 'go and learn'... famous rabbinic statement that the Pharisees probably used often. 'I desire mercy...' - Jesus thinks that the Pharisees are so intent on the 'sacrificial' religious aspect of following God, that they have missed the bigger heart of the issue: God has always been about mercy and grace. And he quotes from the OT to prove it - this Torah you are trying to follow so closely actually tells you to do what I'm doing!

### Matt. 9:9-13 - Theological Context

Based on the historical and literary contexts, what do we see as the theological issues that are being dealt with? What does this passage tell us about God, Jesus, faith? We have Jesus calling a tax collector (political traitor) to 'follow him', and then eating and socialising with social outcast 'sinners'. The Pharisees (religious people) think it is exactly these groups who are holding back God's blessing, the reason for Roman rule in Judea. In one afternoon, Jesus radically subverts the political, social, and religious dimensions of Jewish society in his day. Jesus is a truly radical figure...

### Examples of historical/cultural context:

Geographical/archaeological context: In the letter to the church at Laodicea (Revelation 3:14-22), the Lord condemns it for being 'neither cold nor hot' and states that 'I wish you were one or the other'. The temptation is to interpret this using our 21st century ideas. This would lead us to conclude that the Lord would prefer 'cold' Christians to 'lukewarm' Christians. Archaeological studies have shown that Laodicea was located near to hot springs at Hierapolis and a cold stream near Colossae. The point is both hot and cold are desirable for different purposes. However, the spiritual condition of the church at Laodicea was more like the tepid lukewarm water that flowed into the Laodicean pipes. It was neither hot nor cold. Political/social context: In Daniel 5:7, King Belshazzar offered the third position in government to Daniel. Why didn't he offer the second? Secular history tells us that Belshazzar was second in command and that his father Nabonidus was out of the country for an extended period of time. Thus the highest Daniel could be given was third. In Philippians 3:20, Paul refers to his readers' citizenship in heaven. This makes more sense to us when we understand that Philippi was a Roman colony and that the emperor had given all Philippian citizens 'Italic' rights, i.e. they had the same rights as a citizen of Rome. Knowing this, Paul wrote of an even greater citizenship for the Christians in Philippi, i.e. their citizenship of heaven. This would have had special meaning to the initial readers of the epistle. Cultural context: Secular sources show us that the Ninevites treated their enemies in an appalling way. They beheaded the leaders of peoples they conquered and piled up their heads. Sometimes, a leader was captured and put in a cage. Often they impaled their captives - a horrible death. Other times, they stretched out the legs and arms of a captive and skinned him alive. This shows us why Jonah did not want to preach a message of repentance to the Ninevites. He felt they deserved judgment for their atrocities. In Luke 13:32, Jesus calls Herod Antipas a 'fox'. To us, a fox is very cunning and slick. However, in biblical times, fox was low in cunning: it was treacherous and insignificant when compared to a lion. We can't interpret words and phrases according to what they mean to us. Note also that the study of historical/cultural context will often overlap with the literary/grammatical context.

## 3. Thematic Analysis Matrices
### Theme: Historical-Critical Method
The document traces the rise of the historical-critical method from the Modern period (1800-), noting its reliance on scientific, objective approaches that bracket out faith to focus on historical background and source documents. It highlights the eventual loosening of this method's 'stranglehold' in the 20th century.

### Theme: Sola Scriptura
A central tenet of the Reformation, where Luther and Calvin asserted that Scripture has divine authority and is its own best interpreter, rejecting traditional allegorical methods in favor of a plain, historical sense.

### Theme: Theological Interpretation
The document advocates for a balanced approach that utilizes historical tools while maintaining allegiance to God, aiming for edification and instruction rather than purely academic or rationalistic analysis.

### Theme: Contextualization
The text emphasizes the necessity of understanding historical, cultural, and literary contexts (e.g., the example of Matt 9:9-13 and the Laodicean church) to avoid misinterpreting biblical passages through modern lenses.
