# The Treasury of David: Psalm XXXVII
**Author:** [[C.H. Spurgeon]]

## 1. Verbatim Source Text
### Psalm XXXVII

TITLE. - Of David. - There is but this word to denote the authorship; whether it was a song or a meditation we are not told. It was written by David in his old age (verse 25), and is the more valuable as the record of so many years' experience. SUBJECT. - This is the great Psalm of the wicked and the affliction of the righteous. It is written in many ways, it is never dealt with in the light of the future; and perhaps no Psalm is more constantly in the heart of the believer. It is a Psalm in which the Lord's people are repining at the most impressively forbidden. It is a Psalm in which the Lord's people are constantly the too common repining of his people, and the Lord's people are surrounded. It contains dealings with his own chosen, illustrated by the way in which they are surrounded. It abounds in eight alphabetical, or nearly so, stanzas, illustrated by autobiographical statements, and abounds in eight-line contrasts. DIVISION. - The Psalm can scarcely be divided into considerable sections. It is really a chapter of the book of Proverbs, made of the various things that come to themselves. It is alphabetical. It is not, however, in strict order, the first letters of the verses follow the Hebrew alphabet. They have been not only a poetical invention, but a help to memory. The reader is requested to read the Psalm through without comment before he turns to the exposition.

### Exposition

FRET not thyself because of evildoers, neither be thou envious against the workers of iniquity. 2 For they shall soon be cut down like the grass, and wither as the green herb. 1. The Psalm opens with this first precept. It is alas! too common to believers in their hours of adversity to think themselves harshly dealt with, when they see those utterly destitute of religion and honesty, rejoicing in worldly prosperity. Much needed is the command, 'Fret not thyself because of evildoers.' To fret is to worry, to have the heart-burn, when it sees it is vexed. Nature is very apt to kindle a fire of discontent when it sees the breakers riding on horses, and the ungodly walking in the mire: the best lesson learned in the school of grace, when one comes to view the men of the world, is to silence all the devout complacency of one who is saintly. The Lord is righteous in all his acts. It seems hard to carnal judgment that the best meat should go to the dogs, while loving children, perhaps, have none. 'Neither be thou envious against the workers of iniquity.' The same after Adam naturally shape. When one is poor, then they, and in deep trial, our all under another shape. When one of the rich and great; and when we are to hand with blasphemous reasonings. Stony weather may curdle even the cream of adversity. Evil men instead of being envied, are to be objects of horror and aversion; yet their loaded tables, and gilded ceilings, are too apt to fascinate our poor half-formed souls. Why should the fat bullock of ribbons and garlands, which decorate him as he is led to the beasts fatted for the slaughter, parallel one for ungodly rich men but as beasts fatted for the slaughter? 2. 'For they shall soon be cut down like the grass.' The scythe of death is sharpening. Green grows the grass, but the mower is near. The destruction of the ungodly will be sudden, sure, overwhelming, and complete. 'The grass cannot escape the mower, and wither as the green herb.' The sun of the day of the herb dries up at once in the heat of the sun, and all the glory of the wicked shall disappear at the hour of death. Death is the ungodly man like grass, and wrath withers him like hay; he dies, and name rots. How complete an end is made of the man whose boasts had no end! Is it worth while to waste ourselves in fretting about the insect of an hour, an ephemera which in the same day is born and dies? Within believers there is a living and incorruptible seed which liveth and abideth for ever; why should they envy mere flesh, and the glory of it, which are but as grass, and wither thereof?

## 3. Thematic Analysis Matrices
### Theme: The Folly of Envy
Spurgeon argues that believers should not fret or envy the wicked because their prosperity is fleeting and their end is certain. He compares the wicked to grass that is soon cut down by the 'scythe of death'.

### Theme: Divine Providence
The text emphasizes that the Lord is righteous in all His acts and that the believer's duty is to trust in Him, even when the wicked seem to prosper. The author asserts that the righteous have an 'incorruptible seed' that abides forever.
