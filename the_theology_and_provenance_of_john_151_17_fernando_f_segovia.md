# The Theology and Provenance of John 15:1-17
**Author:** [[Fernando F. Segovia]]

## 1. Verbatim Source Text
### Introduction

ONE of the most frequently recurring problems in Johannine research has been and continues to be the present structure and composition of the farewell discourse of Jesus (13:31-17:26). Nowadays hardly any exegete would maintain that these chapters constitute a literary unity in their present form. The most convincing objection against such a position lies in the clearly concluding statement of 14:31c, "Arise, let us depart from here." Yet, it is precisely at this point that Jesus unexpectedly launches into a new discourse, which, if taken as a unity, is far more extensive than the first discourse of 13:31-14:31. Furthermore, an examination of the beginning of chap. 18 shows that vv 1-2 can pick up very well exactly where 14:31c leaves off, both sequentially and contextually. From the point of view of context, the narrative returns to Judas's betrayal-it was Judas's departure in 13:30 that signalled the beginning of the first discourse. Likewise, from the point of view of sequence, the command of Jesus of 14:31c is easily continued by the statement of 18:1 concerning Jesus' movement: "After he had finished speaking, Jesus proceeded with his disciples across the Kidron valley, . . ." Thus, in the light of these connections between 13:31-14:31 and chap. 18, Johannine scholars have attempted to give a satisfactory explanation of the present position in the gospel of the intervening chapters, i.e., chaps. 15-17. The proposed solutions may be divided into four different schools or lines of interpretation, which I shall call the historicizing, transpositional, softening, and redactional approaches.

### I. Delineation of John 15:1-17

The beginning of the first addition or originally independent discourse consists of the introduction of the figure of the vine and the branches, i.e., 15:1-2 The figure is totally unexpected after the concluding command of 14:31c The conclusion of the unit, on the other hand, should be placed at v 17 for the following reasons (a) the development of this figure ends with v 17 and presents a very clear and discernible structure; (b) the exposition of the love command of 15:9-17 is not continued after v 17, (c) the theme of the hatred of the world for the disciples, which becomes primary after v 18, is not anticipated at all in the preceding verses; and (d) the development of this new theme, which extends through 16:4a, is made quite independently of 15:1-17 and presents a very clear and discernible structure as well. As a result, 15:1-17 should be seen as the first literary unit in John 15-17.

### II. Structure of Jn. 15:1-17

This first discourse of John 15-17 may be divided, in my opinion, into two major subsections. The first one consists of vv 1-8 and centers on the development of the imagery of the vine and the branches of vv 1-2. The second subsection, consisting of vv 9-17, leaves that imagery completely behind and turns instead to "love" (agape) as an explanation of the imagery-the theme of love now opens and closes the rest of the discourse. One should notice above all the reprise of the command of v 4 ("Abide in me") in v 9 ("Abide in my love"); the change of themes is thus clearly outlined.

### III. Exegesis of 15:1-8

This first subsection of the discourse may be further subdivided into four main components: (a) the introduction of the figure of the vine and the branches, vv 1-2; (b) a statement concerning the basic origin of all the branches, v 3; (c) an exhortation to the branches to "abide in" (menein en) the vine, vv 4-7; and (d) the result of such an abiding, v 8. In what follows, I shall concentrate primarily on vv 1-2 and 4-7. (a) At the very beginning of the discourse, four identifications are made-two explicit; two implicit-between the figures of viticulture and the community situation. The two explicit examples are found in v 1: a typically Johannine "I am" saying identifies Jesus as "the true vine" (he ampelos he alethine), while the Father is described as "the gardener" (ho georgos). V 2 provides the two implicit examples: a disciple is a "branch" (pan klema) and his task is one of "bearing fruit" (to karpon pherein).

### IV. Exegesis of vv 15:9-17

As already mentioned above, "love" now becomes and remains the dominant theme of the rest of the discourse. This second subsection may be further subdivided into three main components: (a) the introduction of the different relationships of love and their hierarchy, vv 9-10; (b) a statement concerning the joy of the believers, v 11; and (c) the exposition of the love command, vv 12-17. In what follows, I shall concentrate primarily on vv 9-10 and 12-17. (a) Structurally, the beginning of the second subsection parallels to a great extent that of the first. Thus, for example, v 9a presents the three characters from the figure of vv 1-2-the Father, the disciples and Jesus-once again closely united, although now in a series of relationships of love. Furthermore, v 9b resumes the command of v 4 with its emphasis on the need to "abide," but now phrases it in terms of the theme of love ("Abide in my love"). First of all, the relationships of love of v 9a establish a hierarchyical order of love: just as (and "because") the Father loved Jesus, so Jesus loved the disciples. The hierarchy, in descending order, is as follows: the Father; Jesus; the disciples. Secondly, just as the "branches" are urged to abide in the "vine," i.e., in the word of Jesus by which they have been cleansed, so now the believers are urged to abide in the love of Jesus which they have experienced. This command indicates that a situation similar to that described in the first subsection should be presupposed behind v 9 as well; some believers are not abiding in the love of Jesus, in the hierarchy. Thus a new aspect of the inner-Christian problem is introduced.

### V. Summary and Conclusions

In the light of the preceding analysis, each subsection of this discourse may be seen as presenting a different aspect of an inner-Christian or intra-church controversy. The first subsection, 15:1-8, shows that members of the community have either ceased or are in danger of ceasing to abide as "branches." Further, the author's insistence on abiding in Jesus (vv 4 [2], 5, 6, 7) or in the vine (v 4) and the emphasis on Jesus as "the true vine" (v 1) indicate that the problem at hand is partly christological in nature and that such a problem consists in the development of an understanding of the Christ different from that which had been originally received and accepted. The second subsection, 15:9-17, shows that members of the community have either ceased or are in danger of ceasing to abide as "friends" in the hierarchy. Further, the author's insistence on fulfilling Jesus' "commands" and specifically the command to love one another indicates that the problem at hand is also partly ethical in nature. Thus, the inner-Christian problem reflected in 15:1-17 has both christological and ethical connotations. In conclusion, I would argue that John 15:1-17 shares the theological concerns and Sitz im Leben of 1 John. In both texts Christian believers have deviated from the original belief of the community through the adoption of a different christology, showing decidedly docetic connotations, and a different ethical position, showing a disregard for the command of mutual love. In both writings, furthermore, the plan of attack adopted against these believers is the same: an insistence on the love command-against the ethical deviations-and an inclusion of the acceptance of Jesus' death within that love command-against the christological deviations. Therefore, I believe that the discourse was written either by the author of 1 John or by someone in the same situation and that it was then added on to the gospel after 13:31-14:31.

## 3. Thematic Analysis Matrices
### Theme: Literary Unity of the Farewell Discourse
The author argues that the farewell discourse (13:31-17:26) is not a literary unity, citing the concluding statement of 14:31c as a major break and noting that chapter 18 follows 14:31c more naturally than chapter 15 does.

### Theme: Inner-Christian Controversy
The author posits that John 15:1-17 addresses an internal community crisis rather than external conflict with Judaism. This is evidenced by the lack of mention of the synagogue or the Jews, focusing instead on believers who are failing to 'abide' in the vine or in the love of Jesus.

### Theme: Christological and Ethical Deviations
The author identifies two primary problems in the community: a christological deviation (docetic tendencies) and an ethical deviation (disregard for the command of mutual love). These are addressed by the author's insistence on abiding in the vine and the command to love one another.
