# 4 John
**Author:** [[Unknown]]

## 1. Verbatim Source Text
### Jesus and Nicodemus (3:1-15)

This is the first of some highly important discourses between Jesus and people from widely different backgrounds (Nicodemus, the Samaritan woman, the Gentile official, the man at Bethesda). Sometimes the discourse and the signs are intimately connected. Nicodemus, as a Pharisee, would have stressed careful observance of the law and the traditions of the elders in order to obtain salvation. By including this discourse, John shows us that this belief is not correct. A radical rebirth is required for salvation. Nicodemus held a high position in Judaism, being a Pharisee, a ruler of the Jews and, in Jesus' words, the teacher of Israel. Nicodemus came at night. Why? 1. possibly because it would have been a chance to have an uninterrupted conversation 2. out of fear - the dark would give him a degree of anonymity 3. John uses light and dark in a spiritual way in this Gospel. Darkness here could indicate the spiritual darkness of Nicodemus. Nicodemus calls Jesus 'Rabbi' - this is likely to reflect his belief that Jesus came from God. There is no indication that he saw Jesus as divine or Messiah. Nicodemus also says 'we know' probably showing that other Pharisees or members of the Sanhedrin shared his view. Nicodemus also mentions 'signs' even though John has recorded only one 'sign' to this point. Spiritual birth Jesus' response to Nicodemus in verse 3 shows that, even though Nicodemus believes he can discern spiritual things (e.g. see that Jesus is from God from the evidence of His signs), the saving reign of God cannot be seen unless a person is born again. What does Jesus mean by the 'kingdom of God'? This expression is not found in the OT. However, the prophets foresaw the coming of a kingdom at the end of history ruled by a son of David or the Lord's servant or the Lord Himself (all three meaning the same thing). For a Jew like Nicodemus, to see the kingdom of God was to participate in the end-time kingdom, to have resurrection and eternal life. The Jews thought that all but extremely wicked Jews would enter the kingdom. Jesus is saying to Nicodemus that even a Jew must be born again to enter the kingdom. Even though the kingdom of God is eschatological (i.e. will come about at the end of the age), both the Synoptics and John show that a person can participate in eternal life now ('already but not yet'). The kingdom of God is God's rule over His creation and His people. It exists where He rules (in the hearts of believers) and will ultimately take physical form at the end of the age. God's kingdom is 'reign' not 'realm'. What does born again mean? Born comes from a Greek word which can refer to the act of the father (to beget) or the mother (to give birth to). The idea here is generation or regeneration. 'Again' comes from a Greek word which can mean 'again' or 'from above'. Both are relevant as regeneration is an act of God. Jesus is telling Nicodemus that a person must be regenerated by God before the end of the world in order to enter the kingdom of God.

### Heavenly things

Nicodemus did not understand Who Jesus was and failed to understand His witness. He did not believe in Jesus as Son of God and Messiah. The 'earthly things' Jesus refers to are His teachings on the new birth. They are earthly as they must take place in human beings while they are on earth. The 'heavenly things' are those things relating to the eschatological kingdom which Jesus deals with in verses 13ff. Jesus is probably referring to Jewish stories of people who had ascended to heaven and received insight and returned to speak of heavenly things. Jesus can speak of heavenly things because heaven was His home in the first place, not because he was on earth and went to heaven and came back. Note that the ascension is in the past tense - this is probably a statement by Jesus of what will happen. It cannot be a reference to a past event as the resurrection has not happened at this point. The bronze snake (Numbers 21:4-9) was how God gave physical life to the Israelites. It was His gracious provision of new life. It is therefore not strange that He gives new spiritual life. As with the bronze snake, Jesus would be lifted up - this refers both to the cross and exaltation. John sees the crucifixion and resurrection of Jesus as parts of the one redemptive event. Those who turn to Jesus as the Israelites turned to the snake will receive new life. The purpose of Jesus being lifted up is that those who believe in Him will have eternal life. This is the answer to Nicodemus' question 'how can this happen' - new birth is experienced through the saving work of Christ on the cross, received through belief/faith.

### John's Commentary (3:16-21)

It seems likely that these verses are explanatory ones by John and do not form part of the discussion between Jesus and Nicodemus. Authors disagree on where the discussion ends and where John's commentary begins. v16 The mission of the Son is grounded in the love of God. The Greek emphasises the intensity of the love. The fact that He gave his one and only Son shows the greatness of the gift. The act of giving is one of sacrifice. The Jews knew that God loved Israel but John shows His love encompasses all races. God loved the kosmos - this word for 'world' usually has negative connotations - it shows God's grace. He loved the world despite its badness. 'Believe' here is not intellectual assent. It means trusting, having complete faith in Christ. The consequence of unbelief is clear - those who do not believe will perish eternally. v17 John clarifies the purpose of the mission of the Son. He came not to condemn but to save. 'Condemn' can also mean 'judge'. Jesus states in chapter 9:39 'For judgment I have come into this world...' How can this be reconciled? It is clear in 3:17 that judgment refers to condemnation. In 9:39, judgment is more neutral, not necessarily meaning condemnation. Also, salvation and judgment are two sides of the same coin. Jesus came to save but it is clear from the following verses that not all will accept Him - these will be condemned. Consequently, His earthly mission to save also results

### Extended Comment (3:31-36)

aware, however, that the NT church would see Christ as the bridegroom and the church as the bride, continuing and transforming the OT theme. John's joy comes from the supremacy of Christ. He was not a rival to Jesus but a forerunner and it was time for his ministry to end as Jesus' progressed. Note that John says that he must decrease and Jesus must increase. There is no choice here. As with 3:16-21, these verses seem to be John's reflections, explaining the previous verses, rather than the words of John the Baptist. Why must Jesus become greater? Jesus alone is from above and is therefore above all. All others are from the earth. 'Earth' is not kosmos and does not refer to sinfulness but to the fact that humans are limited and finite. John, as one from the earth, speaks as one from the earth and therefore must become less. He can not reveal the things of heaven nor can he offer new birth from above. In contrast, Jesus testifies to heavenly things but no one accepts His testimony. John is simply repeating Jesus' own evaluation. The believer accepting Jesus' testimony By accepting Jesus' testimony as to what He has seen and heard, the believer has certified that God is truthful. This is so because the One God has sent speaks the words of God. Jesus has received the Spirit without limit and His words are true. Other interpretations are possible here but are unlikely. In verse 33, the NIV has omitted the word 'seal'. The NASB says 'He who has received His witness has set his seal to this, that God is true'. The seal here authenticates that God is true (not as in the NIV that God is truthful). Man gives his personal guarantee that God is true, i.e. that the God's divine revelation in Christ is true. Father and Son (34-36) Although the Father sends the Son and the Son is the One sent and the Son obeys the Father, not the reverse, their relationship is nevertheless one of love. Because of this love, the Father has given the Spirit to Him without measure and has placed everything in His hands. The Father has placed even His plan of salvation into the Son's hands. The Son is the perfect spokesman and revealer of the Father. In the context, 'all things' refers especially to the gift of life in the Spirit. Men can come to Christ and be saved. Verse 36 is the climax to the whole chapter. There are only two alternatives - genuine faith and defiant disobedience. The NIV 'rejects' should read 'disobeys'. Belief/faith in the Son is the only way to eternal life. All who do not believe stand already condemned (verse 18). God's wrath is already looming over them. God's wrath in this verse refers to God's personal response to unbelief. He will deal with each unbeliever personally.

## 3. Thematic Analysis Matrices
### Theme: The Necessity of Rebirth
The text argues that salvation is not obtained through legalistic observance of the law, but through a radical spiritual rebirth. This rebirth is an act of God, described as being born 'from above'.

### Theme: The Kingdom of God
The document defines the kingdom of God as God's reign over His creation and people, existing in the hearts of believers now ('already but not yet') and reaching its ultimate physical form at the end of the age.

### Theme: The Nature of Faith
The text distinguishes between intellectual assent and true faith, defining 'believe' as trusting and having complete faith in Christ, which is the only path to eternal life.

### Theme: The Supremacy of Christ
The document emphasizes that Jesus is from above and above all, serving as the perfect revealer of the Father, while human figures like John the Baptist must decrease as Christ increases.
