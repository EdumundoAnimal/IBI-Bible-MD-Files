# The Atonement Controversy in Welsh Theological Literature & Debate 1707-1841
**Author:** [[Owen Thomas]]

## 1. Verbatim Source Text
### Translator's Introduction

FROM THE TIME OF ITS PUBLICATION in 1874, Owen Thomas' biography of the eminent Welsh preacher, John Jones of Talsarn, Cofiant John Jones, Talsarn (The Life of John Jones, Talsarn, Wrexham: Hughes and Son, 1874), obtained a place of near unique distinction in Welsh Literature. The book's success was immediate, and it has continued to be acknowledged generally as the best biography ever written in Welsh. In the opinion of J. E. Daniel (1902-62), Professor of Christian Doctrine and Philosophy of Religion at Bala-Bangor Theological College, no Welshman can consider himself to be cultured unless he has read it. There is one unusual feature of the biography which has given it special interest ever since its publication: no less than a third of the volume consists of background material on the theological controversies in Wales. Professor Daniel also expressed the commonly held opinion that this material provides the best source for the history of theology in Wales for the period under consideration. Saunders Lewis, Wales' most eminent literary critic of the twentieth century, refers to the context in which this theological material is found, while describing the volume as: A great book because of the greatness of its design, a completely different design from that of the general Welsh biographical tradition. The latter's method was to enclose the subject like a sword in its sheath. To isolate and analyse him as an object on its own, with no essential connection with anything about it, and to hurry over a quick review of his career so as to concentrate on a portrayal of him as a static, unchanging type, with no mention of his relationships with others. Owen Thomas' view of the mutually tolerant position after many years of turmoil is historically inaccurate. Such might have been the appearance when Thomas was writing in the late 1860s in that fierce clashes and denunciations from pulpit and press were over, but that was surely due more to weariness of debate and increasing Victorian respectability than to any real stability of theological positions. As already discussed, the current of thought was drifting ever faster in a liberal, Arminian direction. We conclude this introduction therefore by considering the developments in Calvinistic thinking in Wales from 1841 (when Thomas review ends) to the end of the century. The overall tendency of the period is aptly described by the following comments of W. T. Owen from his life of Edward Williams, Rotherham: Today [Edward Williams] is practically unknown, whereas men of smaller stature are much better known. The key to the solution of this mystery is supplied by Dale when he speaks this of Moderate Calvinists: 'They thought... they could modify some of the Calvinistic doctrines which in their rigid form had become incredible to them. But they were attempting an impossible task, and doing an injustice to the constructive genius of their great master. They had not learnt that theologians who begin with Calvin must end with Calvin. "Moderate Calvinism" was Calvinism in decay. The old Calvinistic phrases, the old Calvinistic definitions, were still on the lips of the independents when George II died, but in the spirit and tendency of their theology they were Calvinists no longer.' Another writer in a similar strain expressed the opinion that the historical significance of 'modified Calvinism' is that it was a 'polite dismissal of Calvinism'. Williams failed to foresee this event, and did not realize that in moderating Calvinism he was only setting in motion a process over the development of which he would have no control, a process which would bring the reign of Calvinism to an end. That is precisely what happened. The moderating process was carried on much further than Williams ever intended it to go; his standpoint was left behind and even forgotten, as congregations became increasingly broader and more liberal in their theology; Eventually it was taken to the point where Calvinism had virtually been abandoned altogether. Prominent in this broader concept of the gospel was the emphasis laid on the responsibility, and worth, and freedom of the individual, which had superseded the idea of man's inadequacy for his own salvation that had figured so largely in Calvinism. Personal godliness rested as much on man's free choice as God's free gift of grace; the gospel was preached as God's love offering pardon and peace to man everywhere, and calling him to choose salvation. What occurred amongst the English Congregationalists in the 1820s, repeated itself amongst the Welsh Calvinistic Methodists by the 1860s. It is a point of some-historical and theological mystery why such a generation in Wales, brought up on a long-standing tradition of classical Reformed teaching, preached to them and by them, with such relevance and vitality, should have sold their birthright with so little struggle over such a short period of time. Any complete explanation would involve a variety of factors, but amongst the Calvinists of the period the following three factors were crucial: i. The Governmental Theory of the Atonement This is perhaps the most important of the modifications of Calvinism to which W. T. Owen refers above. Warfield describes the theory, put forward by Grotius early in the seventeenth century, as a 'half-way house for those who, while touched by the chilling breath of rationalism, have not yet been ready to surrender any semblance of an "objective atonement". He names Godet from continental Europe, Ralph Wardlaw, R. W. Dale and Marcus Dods from Great Britain and Joseph Bellamy, Samuel Hopkins, Jonathan Edwards, Jr. and Albert Barnes from America, as being its chief proponents. [This theory] ... holds that because of his absolute sovereignty God is able to relax at will the demands of the law and to forgive men freely without any expiation or sacrifice for sin, but that in order to preserve a fair degree of discipline and respect for law that men shall not be encouraged to believe that they can commit sin with impunity, He must at the same time give some exhibition of the high esteem which He sets upon the law. The primary purpose in the suffering of Christ or horror for sin ... to secure man's reformation by inducing in him a horror for sin through the awful spectacle of Christ on the cross. With that spectacle before their eyes men were to be made to understand what a serious thing sin really is, that it will not be allowed to go unpunished, and so induced to maintain respect for divine government even in the face of repeated acts of executive clemency. Much of the history of how this view crept into Calvinistic circles in America, England, Scotland and Wales is given by Owen Thomas in the following pages. Although he evidently sees, and criticizes, its weaknesses, yet an element of sympathy towards it cannot be missed, and any awareness that it itself would very rapidly be superseded by a view of atonement possessing no element of satisfaction whatsoever is completely lacking. The theory functioned as the wooden horse to Welsh Calvinism's Troy and carried Liberalism within the gates. ii. The Enthusiasm for a Liberal Education Another factor in the decline of Calvinism, ironically and sadly, was the effect of the work of Lewis Edwards in setting up colleges, from 1837 onwards, for the ministerial students of the Calvinistic Methodists. This, of course, he was supported wholeheartedly by Owen Thomas and by all the denominational leaders of the day. The sense of the need for the highest education possible for these young men was right and good, the sadness was that the necessary establishments were being set up exactly when the prevailing tide in theology was the German spirit of Higher Criticism. The enthusiasm of the times for education resulted in the embracing of modern developments all too uncritically. Wales had no native tradition of Reformed teaching, education and writing to stand as a bulwark against the Higher Critical invasion of the late nineteenth century and as the new ideas arrived they were accepted and propagated with very little consciousness of their novelty and their insidious nature. If, on occasion, they were viewed with suspicion, the likelihood is that there was no awareness of, nor any expertise in, the scholarly means by which they might be combated. iii. The Acceptance of American Methods of Evangelism The influence of Finney's writings from 1839 onwards has already been mentioned. As the century progressed this influence would be deepened by the contribution of preachers returning home after periods of ministry with the Welsh churches in America. The best known of these was Humphrey R. Jones (1832-95), whose preaching on his return to Cardiganshire in 1858 is seen as the beginning of that movement which became the 1859 Revival in Wales. It is significant that during the period he itinerated an evangelist in America, where Finney himself had first come to public notice thirty years before, where Finney Certainly Jones' methods were very much based on Finney's teachings. In a letter of August 1858, at the outset of the revival, he describes his practice: I happened to return from America five weeks ago in order to visit my relations. My friends, on hearing of the great Revival in America and seeing me name associated with the Awakening in the periodicals, greatly desired me to take up the same plan in this region. This I did, with great effect... I receive letters from all parts asking me to organize and conduct similar meetings... I believe that a blessed season is dawning upon Wales and that revivals will become commonplace throughout the land. Towards the close of the nineteenth century the American preacher most admired in Wales was D. L. Moody, and it was his British campaigns, with Ira D. Sankey, in 1873 and 1883 that were to be the patterns for 'revivals' and 'rather than on, rather revivals will become commonplace throughout the land. What answer does the great body of Calvinists give to the question? [For whom did Christ die?] Without doubt, their first answer would be that Christ died for all. It would be folly for any to query the truth of this response for it is taught clearly in the Holy Scriptures. [He then quotes John 3:16, 17, John 2:2, John 1:29, and 1 Tim. 2:6]. We have no part in limiting the unambiguous statements of the Bible. Because the divine revelation teaches that Christ died for all, we Calvinistic-Methodists have in our heart always offered salvation to all. Our being Calvinists does not hinder us in our public proclamation of a salvation that is in extent as all humanity. Perhaps one or two preachers in the old days - the days of the religious controversies - more careful of their Calvinism than of the nature of their hearers, taught a limited Atonement and a particular redemption. ...[but] we will have no part in any limitation of the Atonement. It is an infinite Atonement and, as such, it is an Atonement for the whole world. We rather suspect that we differ a little here from Dr. Lewis Edwards. He taught the sufficiency of it in part is an Atonement for the whole world. the universality of the Atonement. But we hold to both - its sufficiency and its universality. In our judgement its sufficiency ensures its universality... Dr. Lewis Edwards, and all Calvinistic divines of any note, used to say that the Atonement had made salvation of all men possible. What did they mean by this statement? This - that the death of Christ was a sufficient Atonement for all, that no greater or worthier Atonement is needed were all to be saved without distinction. They defended and proclaimed categorically the possibility of the salvation of the whole world by virtue of the Atonement of the Cross, that there were no obstacles on God's part to the salvation of all. But they were not all as categoric and open as one would wish in their proclaiming that there are no obstacles either on man's part, other than his own unwillingness. In their systems, God did very little, if anything, to break down man's unwillingness or to heal his moral inability, inherent due to the original corruption of his nature, to exercise faith in the Saviour. But it is clear to all that it is not enough to teach that there is a sufficient remedy in the Atonement to heal all the sufferers of the Fall, if they are unable to stretch out a hand to receive that remedy - they will die just as surely as if none existed. The Atonement makes it possible on God's part to save all the hearers of the Gospel. Does it also make it possible on men's part? Does it remove man's natural inability to believe? Calvinistic divines have not been so clear and open on this as might be desired. We therefore venture to reply that it does. By that which is known as 'Common Grace' it removes the innate inability of sinners to believe. Both Calvinism and Wesleyans acknowledge this Grace. Dr. Hodge's Systematic Theology contains a chapter on it, though he does not give it as much attention nor as much emphasis as Wesleyan theologians... the Atonement has ensured Common Grace for all - the secret influence of God's Spirit on all hearers of the Gospel - sufficient to remove the moral weakness which results from original sin, and sufficient to enable them to believe in Christ when he is presented to them as a Saviour. No-one in Gospel lands is condemned because he cannot believe, but because he will not though he might... It is an influence upon them, not an influence in them - God's Spirit working on their minds and pushing them in the direction of faith; and the only reason why they will not believe is the stubbornness of their wills. Not inability but unwillingness... This is the reason, although often possibly quite unconsciously, why we invite unbelievers to believe and come to Christ. ## 3. Thematic Analysis Matrices
### Theme: Governmental Theory of the Atonement
The text identifies this as a 'half-way house' modification of Calvinism, where Christ's death is viewed as an exhibition of God's esteem for the law rather than a direct satisfaction of justice, intended to induce reformation and respect for divine government.

### Theme: Sufficiency vs. Universality
The author argues that Calvinists hold to both the sufficiency and universality of the Atonement, asserting that Christ died for all and that the Atonement makes salvation possible for all, while addressing the tension between divine provision and human unwillingness.

### Theme: Common Grace
The text posits that 'Common Grace' is the mechanism by which God removes the innate inability of sinners to believe, shifting the focus from 'inability' to 'unwillingness' as the primary reason for rejection of the Gospel.
